## The core statement

A quintic equation always has five complex roots. What does **not** exist is a single general formula for all quintics using only arithmetic operations and finitely many radicals. The group-theoretic reason: a generic quintic has Galois group $S_5$, which is not a solvable group. [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/QuinticEquation.html)

**Why it matters.** Part 1 argued by counting that $A_5$ has no seams. This post upgrades the picture to the *precise* criterion — solvable by radicals if and only if solvable Galois group — and shows exactly where the general quintic's symmetry chain gets stuck.

## 1. Roots versus formulas

For

$$f(x)=x^5+a_4x^4+a_3x^3+a_2x^2+a_1x+a_0,$$

the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees five roots in $\mathbb C$, counted with multiplicity. The question Abel and Galois settled is different:

> Can every root be expressed from the coefficients using $+,-,\times,\div$, and repeated $n$-th roots?

For degrees 2, 3, and 4, yes. For degree 5 in general, no. Some individual quintics are nevertheless solvable by radicals. [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/QuinticEquation.html)

## 2. The Galois group

Let $K$ be the field generated by the coefficients, often $K=\mathbb Q$, and put all roots of $f$ into a splitting field $L$. The **Galois group**

$$\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$$

consists of the field automorphisms of $L$ that fix the coefficients pointwise. Each automorphism permutes the roots, so the group embeds as a subgroup of $S_5$ — the group is literally *the symmetries of the roots*.

## 3. The main theorem

A central theorem of Galois theory says, in characteristic zero:

$$\boxed{f \text{ is solvable by radicals} \iff \operatorname{Gal}(L/K) \text{ is a solvable group}.}$$

The direction to appreciate: radical expressions impose a structural constraint, because each radical step corresponds to a cyclic layer of symmetry (Part 1, Section 4). Their Galois groups can therefore be dismantled in layers whose successive quotients are abelian. That property is exactly group-theoretic solvability.

## 4. Why $S_5$ blocks radicals

A generic degree-5 polynomial has no special symmetry or algebraic relation among its roots, so every permutation of the five roots can occur: its Galois group is

$$S_5.$$

Now inspect the commutator/derived series:

$$S_5^{(0)} = S_5,
\qquad
S_5^{(1)}=[S_5,S_5]=A_5,
\qquad
S_5^{(2)}=[A_5,A_5]=A_5.$$

**Challenge 2:** Every commutator is an even permutation, so $[S_5,S_5]\subseteq A_5$; combine this with the opening challenge to conclude $S_5^{(1)}=A_5$ exactly.

The crucial obstruction is $A_5$ itself:

- $A_5$ is **non-abelian**.
- $A_5$ is **simple**: it has no nontrivial proper normal subgroups (proved by counting in Part 1).
- It is **perfect**: its commutator subgroup is itself, $[A_5,A_5]=A_5$ — simplicity forces this, since the commutator subgroup is normal, and non-abelianness makes it nontrivial.

Thus, once the derived series reaches $A_5$, it never shrinks to the identity:

$$S_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset \cdots$$

So $S_5$ is not solvable. Consequently, a quintic whose Galois group is $S_5$ cannot have its roots expressed by radicals.

## 5. The important qualification

It is wrong to say "quintic roots do not exist" or "no quintic can be solved." The correct statement is:

$$\boxed{\text{There is no radicals-only formula that solves every quintic.}}$$

For example, $x^5-2=0$ is trivially solvable:

$$x=\sqrt[5]{2}\,\zeta_5^k,\qquad k=0,\dots,4.$$

Its Galois group is solvable, unlike $S_5$. A quintic is solvable by radicals precisely when its *particular* Galois group is solvable — not merely because it has degree five. [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/QuinticEquation.html)
