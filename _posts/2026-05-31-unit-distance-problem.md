## 1. The Problem

In 1946, Paul Erdős posed the following question to the mathematical world:

> Given $n$ points in the plane, what is the maximum number of pairs of points that can be exactly distance 1 apart?

Let $u(n)$ denote this maximum. The question is deceptively simple — a child can understand it — yet its asymptotic behavior remains one of the most famous open problems in combinatorial geometry. Erdős offered $500 for a solution, a prize that still stands.

---

## 2. Why It Matters

The Unit Distance Problem sits at the intersection of:

- **Combinatorial geometry** — counting incidences between points and unit circles
- **Graph theory** — unit distance graphs are a natural geometric graph class
- **Additive combinatorics** — the Szemerédi–Trotter theorem provides the best upper bound
- **Number theory** — constructions often exploit algebraic structure in the plane

It is one of those rare problems where the gap between lower and upper bounds has resisted improvement for decades, despite attacks from multiple directions.

---

## 3. Small Values of $u(n)$

For small $n$, exact values of $u(n)$ are known through exhaustive search and geometric reasoning.

| $n$ | $u(n)$ | Optimal Configuration |
|-----|--------|----------------------|
| 1   | 0      | Single isolated point |
| 2   | 1      | Two points distance 1 apart |
| 3   | 3      | Equilateral triangle (all 3 pairs) |
| 4   | 5      | Two equilateral triangles sharing an edge |
| 5   | 7      | Triangular lattice patch |
| 6   | 9      | Regular hexagon (perimeter pairs) |
| 7   | 12     | Hexagon plus center |
| 8   | 14     | Two adjacent hexagons |
| 9   | 18     | $3 \times 3$ triangular lattice |
| 10  | 20     | Extended lattice patch |

### The equilateral triangle: $n = 3$

Points: $(0,0), (1,0), \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$. All three pairwise distances are exactly 1, so $u(3) = 3$.

### The square: $n = 4$

Points: $(0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1)$. Four perimeter edges of length 1, so $u(4) = 5$ (not 4 — the square with diagonal 1 can be arranged differently to get 5 unit pairs).

### The hexagon plus center: $n = 7$

A regular hexagon of side 1 with its center. Six perimeter edges plus six radii yield $u(7) = 12$ unit-distance pairs.

**Challenge:** For $n = 5$, the regular pentagon gives only 5 unit-distance pairs (its perimeter). Can you find a configuration of 5 points achieving more than 5?

## 4. The Triangular Lattice: Nature's Densest Packing

The densest known constructions for large $n$ come from the **triangular lattice**. This is the lattice generated by the basis vectors:

$$
\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0), \qquad \mathbf{e}_2 = \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
$$

Each interior point in this lattice has **6 neighbors** at distance exactly 1 — the maximum possible degree for a unit-distance graph in the plane (a consequence of the fact that a unit circle can contain at most 6 points with pairwise distances $\ge 1$).

Taking a roughly hexagonal patch of the triangular lattice yields the following counts:

| 10  | 18     | | 20  | 55     |
| 11  | 21     | | 21  | 59     |
| 12  | 24     | | 22  | 64     |

| 14  | 31     | | 24  | 73     |
| 15  | 34     | | 25  | 78     |
| 16  | 38     | | 26  | 83     |
| 17  | 42     | | 27  | 88     |

| 19  | 50     | | 29  | 99     |

## 5. Hexagonal Patch Formula

For a regular hexagonal lattice patch of radius $r$, the number of points and unit edges follow exact quadratic formulas:

$$
n = 1 + 3r(r+1)
$$

$$
E = 9r^2 + 3r
$$

| $r$ | $n$ | $u(n)$ |
|-----|-----|--------|
| 1   | 7   | 12     |
| 2   | 19  | 42     |
| 3   | 37  | 90     |
| 4   | 61  | 156    |
| 5   | 91  | 240    |
| 6   | 127 | 342    |
| 7   | 169 | 462    |
| 8   | 217 | 600    |
| 9   | 271 | 756    |
| 10  | 331 | 930    |

This construction grows with $u(n) \approx 3n$ — essentially, each interior point contributes about 3 unit-distance pairs (each edge counted twice, so 6 neighbors per point divided by 2).

## 6. Square Grid

A square $m \times m$ grid with spacing 1 yields:

$$
u(n) = n - m = m^2 - m = m(m-1)
$$

where $n = m^2$. But we can do better by also counting the horizontal edges: $2m(m-1)$ total.

| Grid | $n$ | $u(n)$ |
|------|-----|--------|
| $2 \times 2$ | 4   | 4      |
| $3 \times 3$ | 9   | 12     |
| $4 \times 4$ | 16  | 24     |
| $5 \times 5$ | 25  | 40     |
| $6 \times 6$ | 36  | 60     |
| $7 \times 7$ | 49  | 84     |
| $8 \times 8$ | 64  | 112    |
| $9 \times 9$ | 81  | 144    |
| $10 \times 10$ | 100 | 180  |
| $20 \times 20$ | 400 | 760 |

The square grid gives $u(n) \approx 2n$, which is worse than the triangular lattice's $\approx 3n$. The triangular lattice is denser because each point has 6 neighbors vs. 4 in the square grid.

## 7. Regular Polygons and Other Constructions

### 7.1 Regular polygons of side 1

For a regular $k$-gon with unit side length, we get $u(k) = k$ (all perimeter edges). These are not optimal for $k \ge 5$, but they provide natural lower bounds.

| $k$ | $u(k)$ |
|-----|--------|
| 3 (Triangle) | 3 |
| 4 (Square) | 4 |
| 5 (Pentagon) | 5 |
| 6 (Hexagon) | 6 |
| 7 (Heptagon) | 7 |
| 8 (Octagon) | 8 |
| 10 (Decagon) | 10 |
| 12 (Dodecagon) | 12 |

### 7.2 Triangular number arrangements

When $n = T_k = k(k+1)/2$ (a triangular number), points can be arranged as a triangular patch of the triangular lattice. This yields particularly nice counts:

$$
u(T_k) = \frac{3}{2}T_k - \frac{3}{2}k
$$

expressed in terms of the triangular index $k$.

## 8. Large-Scale Asymptotics

For large $n$, triangular lattice patches give:

$$
u(n) \sim \sqrt{3}\, n \approx 1.732\,n
$$

But this is only a **lower bound**. The true asymptotic growth of $u(n)$ could be larger. A more refined lower bound, due to Erdős, uses number-theoretic constructions:

$$
u(n) \ge n^{1 + c/\log\log n}
$$

for some constant $c > 0$. This beats any $n (\log n)^k$ but is still sub-polynomial in its excess over linear.

Examples of large-$n$ lattice constructions:

| $n$ | Construction |
|-----|-------------|
| 500   | Triangular lattice |
| 1,000 | Triangular lattice |
| 5,000 | Triangular lattice |
| 10,000 | Triangular lattice |
| 50,000 | Triangular lattice |
| 100,000 | Triangular lattice |

## 9. The Best Known Bounds

The current state of the art, as of 2026:

**Lower bound** (Erdős, 1946; improved by J. Solymosi and others):

$$
u(n) \ge n^{1 + c/\log\log n}
$$

This uses points on a $\sqrt{n} \times \sqrt{n}$ grid, counting only those pairs whose squared distance is a sum of two squares in many ways — a number-theoretic construction.

**Upper bound** (Spencer, Szemerédi, Trotter, 1984):

$$
u(n) \le C n^{4/3}
$$

for some absolute constant $C$. This uses the Szemerédi–Trotter incidence theorem: each point determines a unit circle centered at it, and unit-distance pairs correspond to point-circle incidences.

The gap between $n^{1 + o(1)}$ and $n^{4/3}$ has stood for over 40 years. Closing it — in either direction — would be a major breakthrough.

**Challenge:** Why does Szemerédi–Trotter give an $n^{4/3}$ bound? The rough idea: there are $n$ points and $n$ unit circles (one centered at each point). Each point-circle incidence is a unit-distance pair. The Szemerédi–Trotter theorem bounds the number of incidences between $n$ points and $n$ circles of the same radius by $O(n^{4/3})$. Work through the details to convince yourself.

## 10. The Erdős Unit Distance Graph

A related concept is the **unit distance graph**: the graph whose vertices are points in the plane, with edges between points at distance exactly 1.

Key facts:

- The **chromatic number** of the plane (the Hadwiger–Nelson problem) asks: how many colors are needed to color the plane so that no two points at distance 1 have the same color? The answer is known to be 5, 6, or 7.
- The **Moser spindle** is a unit-distance graph with 7 vertices, chromatic number 4, that proves at least 4 colors are needed.
- The maximum degree of any vertex in a unit-distance graph is unbounded — but in any **finite** unit-distance graph, the maximum degree is at most 6 in a triangular lattice arrangement.

---

## 11. Deeper Significance

The Unit Distance Problem is deceptively deep because:

- **Geometric constraints** impose algebraic structure — not every graph is a unit-distance graph
- **Number theory** enters through the algebraic characterization of distances: $d = 1$ means $d^2 = 1$ means $(x_1 - x_2)^2 + (y_1 - y_2)^2 = 1$
- **Incidence geometry** provides the only known route to sub-quadratic upper bounds
- **Extremal combinatorics** asks: what is the densest possible geometric graph avoiding some forbidden subgraph?

The problem connects to the **Erdős distinct distances problem** (how many *distinct* distances must $n$ points determine?), the **Szemerédi–Trotter theorem**, and the **Crossing Number Lemma** — forming a web of results that define modern discrete geometry.
