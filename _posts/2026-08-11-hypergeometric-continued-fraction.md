---

Gauss's continued fraction expresses a ratio of contiguous Gaussian hypergeometric functions as a continued fraction. A standard form is

$$
\frac{ {}_2F_1(a,b+1;c+1;z)}
{ {}_2F_1(a,b;c;z)}
=
\cfrac{1}{
1-\cfrac{
\frac{a(c-b)}{c(c+1)}z
}{
1-\cfrac{
\frac{(b+1)(c-a+1)}{(c+1)(c+2)}z
}{
1-\cfrac{
\frac{(a+1)(c-b+1)}{(c+2)(c+3)}z
}{
1-\cfrac{
\frac{(b+2)(c-a+2)}{(c+3)(c+4)}z
}{\ddots}
}}}}
$$

where

$$
{}_2F_1(a,b;c;z)
=
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{(a)_n(b)_n}{(c)_n\,n!}z^n,
$$

and $$(q)_n=q(q+1)\cdots(q+n-1)$$ is the rising factorial. [math.tugraz](https://www.math.tugraz.at/~prodinger/Avery-contribution-July-2012.pdf)

---

## 1. Why It Matters

It converts a hypergeometric-function ratio into a three-term-recurrence structure. This is useful because continued fractions can be **numerically stable** for evaluating certain function ratios — particularly in parameter or argument ranges where directly summing the power series is slow or ill-conditioned. The identity comes from *contiguous relations*: linear identities among $${}_2F_1$$ functions whose parameters differ by integers. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_continued_fraction)

**Challenge to the reader:** Write the fraction as $$1/(1-a_1z/(1-a_2z/(1-a_3z/(1-\cdots))))$$. Show that the partial numerators $$a_n$$ alternate between two families: one governed by shifts of $$a$$ and $$c-b$$, the other by shifts of $$b+1$$ and $$c-a+1$$. This alternation is the fingerprint of the Gauss contiguous relations.

## 2. Equivalent Sign Convention

You will often see the same fraction written as

$$
\frac{ {}_2F_1(a+1,b;c+1;z)}
{ {}_2F_1(a,b;c;z)}
=
\cfrac{1}{
1+\cfrac{k_1z}{
1+\cfrac{k_2z}{
1+\cfrac{k_3z}{\ddots}}}},
$$

with coefficients alternating as

$$
k_{2m+1}
=
\frac{(a-c-m)(b+m)}
{(c+2m)(c+2m+1)},
\qquad
k_{2m+2}
=
\frac{(b-c-m-1)(a+m+1)}
{(c+2m+1)(c+2m+2)}.
$$

This is the same underlying result, just using a different contiguous ratio and absorbing minus signs into the partial numerators. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_function)

---

## 3. Where It Comes From

The continued fraction is generated by iterating the contiguous relation

$$
F(a,b;c;z) = F(a-1,b;c;z) + \frac{bz}{c}F(a,b+1;c+1;z),
$$

derived in the companion post on contiguous relations. Rearranging gives

$$
\frac{F(a,b+1;c+1;z)}{F(a,b;c;z)} =
\frac{1}{1 - \frac{bz}{c}\frac{F(a-1,b+1;c+1;z)}{F(a,b+1;c+1;z)}}.
$$

But the ratio on the right is the same functional form with shifted parameters — so the process repeats, producing the infinite fraction. Each step shifts one numerator and one denominator parameter, cycling through the alternating pattern visible in the coefficients.

---

## 4. Specializations

The Gauss continued fraction is a **general template** that specializes to continued fractions for familiar functions:

| Function | Parameters | Resulting continued fraction |
|---|---|---|
| $$\log(1+z)$$ | $$a=1,b=0,c=0$$ (limit) | Classical Lambert continued fraction for the logarithm |
| $$\arctan z$$ | $$a=\tfrac12,b=0,c=\tfrac12$$ (limit) | Continued fraction for the inverse tangent |
| Bessel ratios $$J_{\nu+1}(z)/J_\nu(z)$$ | Confluent limit of $${}_2F_1$$ | Gauss's continued fraction for Bessel functions |

[en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_continued_fraction)

Each specialization inherits the alternating coefficient structure from the parent $${}_2F_1$$ fraction — the parameters simply pick out different numerical coefficients.

---

## 5. Numerical Advantage

Why use a continued fraction when you already have the power series? The series for $${}_2F_1(a,b;c;z)$$ converges geometrically for $$\lvert z\rvert \lt 1$$, but convergence slows dramatically near the unit circle. The continued fraction, by contrast, can converge rapidly even **on** the branch cut ($$z\ge 1$$ in the standard normalization) when evaluated with the right backward recurrence (Steed's algorithm or the modified Lentz method). This is why numerical libraries — from SLATEC to SciPy to mpmath — use Gauss's continued fraction (and its confluent and generalized variants) as their primary evaluation engine for hypergeometric functions outside the power-series domain.

---

## 6. Deeper Significance

Gauss's continued fraction is not an isolated curiosity — it is the **convergent shadow** of the three-term recurrence that governs the monodromy of the hypergeometric equation. Every regular-singular ODE produces a continued fraction for the ratio of two local solutions (or two contiguous function evaluations) via the same mechanism: the recurrence that defines the power-series coefficients also defines the Euclidean algorithm for the corresponding formal power series. Gauss's 1813 derivation — decades before Riemann formulated monodromy — recognized that the continued fraction encodes the same data as the differential equation, but in a form better suited to computation.

**Final challenge to the reader:** Take the continued fraction for $$\tan z$$ (which is a confluent limit of Gauss's fraction). Derive its first three convergents and compare them to the Taylor expansion of $$\tan z$$ about $$z=0$$. Which converges faster near the first pole at $$z=\pi/2$$? This is the practical reason continued fractions replace power series in numerical evaluation — they can converge where series diverge.
