Archimedes' *On Spirals* contains 28 propositions. They fall into two strands: the tangents to the spiral, and the areas bounded by the spiral. The curve itself is generated by combining two uniform motions: a ray rotates about a fixed origin while a point moves outward along the ray at constant speed.

In modern polar coordinates,

$$
\boxed{r=a\theta,\qquad a>0,\quad \theta\geq0.}
$$

Let $d=2\pi a$ be the radial distance gained in one complete revolution. The first circle has radius $d$, the second has radius $2d$, and the $n$-th has radius $nd$.

**Why it matters.** The spiral is not a conic. It is the first curve in Greek mathematics whose tangent and area are both analyzed from a single mechanical definition. That analysis produces two famous non-ruler-and-compass constructions: a straight segment equal to a circle's circumference, and the trisection of an arbitrary angle.

## 1. Preliminaries: Propositions 1–11

The first eleven propositions are supporting lemmas, not the principal results about spirals.

| Propositions | Content and purpose |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Proportionality results for uniform motion: distances travelled and elapsed times |
| 3–4 | Comparison and approximation lemmas used in the tangent proofs |
| 5–9 | Auxiliary circle-and-line constructions, including *neusis*-type constructions |
| 10 | A sum-of-squares identity for lengths in arithmetic progression |
| 11 | A more general arithmetic-progression result |

The first two propositions underpin the relation between the rotation angle and the point's distance from the origin. If the rotation and the outward motion are both uniform, then the radius is proportional to the accumulated angle. That is the definition of the spiral in motion language.

### Proposition 10: the sum-of-squares identity

For $u_k=kh$, the identity can be written

$$
3\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_k^2
=(n+1)u_n^2+h\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_k.
$$

It is equivalent to the familiar formula

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6.
$$

This is the arithmetic behind summing the circular-sector approximations used later. The spiral's area is bounded by sectors whose radii form an arithmetic progression; summing their areas is a sum of squares.

## 2. The basic properties: Propositions 12–15

### Proposition 12: equal angles give an arithmetic progression of radii

If successive rays from the origin meet the spiral at equal angular intervals, their lengths form an arithmetic progression:

$$
r_k=a(\theta_0+k\Delta\theta)
=r_0+ka\Delta\theta.
$$

Equal increments of angle produce equal increments of radius. This is the geometric signature of the two uniform motions.

### Proposition 13: a line cannot touch the spiral twice

A straight line tangent to the spiral touches it at only one point. The statement concerns tangency; a tangent line may still intersect another portion of the spiral.

### Propositions 14 and 15: radius is proportional to accumulated angle

For two points on the first turn, their distances from the origin have the same ratio as the arcs swept out on the first circle:

$$
\frac{r_1}{r_2}=\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_2}.
$$

For points on the second turn, one adds a complete circumference to the corresponding first-circle arcs. Writing

$$
\theta=2\pi(n-1)+\phi,
$$

the modern statement is

$$
r=a\bigl(2\pi(n-1)+\phi\bigr).
$$

The completed revolutions must be counted. The direction of a ray alone does not determine the spiral radius.

## 3. The tangent results: Propositions 16–20

### Propositions 16 and 17: the tangent's angle with the radius

These propositions establish the acute and obtuse angle relations between the tangent and the radius at the point of contact. On the forward side of the radius the angle is obtuse; on the backward side it is acute. Together they supply the geometry needed for the tangent-length results.

### Proposition 18: the tangent at the end of the first turn

Let $P$ be the endpoint of the first complete turn, so $OP=d$. Draw the tangent at $P$, and let it meet at $T$ the line through $O$ perpendicular to $OP$. Then

$$
\boxed{OT=2\pi d.}
$$

The intercepted segment equals the circumference of the first circle.

**Mid-post challenge.** Prove Proposition 18 from the parametrization $x=a\theta\cos\theta$, $y=a\theta\sin\theta$. At $\theta=2\pi$, show that the tangent slope is $2\pi$, and that its intersection with the perpendicular diameter is at distance $2\pi d$ from $O$.

### Proposition 19: the end of any complete turn

At the endpoint of the $n$-th turn, $OP=nd$. With the same perpendicular-and-tangent construction,

$$
OT=n(2\pi\,OP)=2\pi n^2d.
$$

In words: the intercepted perpendicular segment equals $n$ times the circumference of the $n$-th circle.

### Proposition 20: an arbitrary point

At any point $P$, construct $T$ by intersecting the tangent at $P$ with the line through the origin perpendicular to $OP$. The intercepted segment equals the circular arc swept through the full accumulated rotation, measured on the circle of radius $OP$:

$$
\boxed{OT=r\theta=\frac{r^2}{a}.}
$$

For a point beyond the first turn, the arc measurement includes the appropriate number of complete circumferences. Propositions 18 and 19 are the complete-turn cases.

## 4. The area approximation: Propositions 21–23

These propositions establish the exhaustion arguments required to compute areas.

| Proposition | Result |
|---|---|
| 21 | The first-turn region can be enclosed between inscribed and circumscribed figures made of circular sectors, with area difference smaller than any prescribed positive area |
| 22 | The analogous approximation for the second complete turn |
| 23 | The construction extends to a general spiral portion |

Their essential content is that the sector approximations can be made arbitrarily close — not merely that one can draw a rough inner and outer approximation. The sectors are the spiral's version of the inscribed triangles in the parabola.

## 5. The exact areas: Propositions 24–28

### Proposition 24: the first-turn area is one third of the first circle

The area enclosed by the first complete turn and the initial line is

$$
\boxed{A_1=\frac13\pi d^2.}
$$

The proposition also treats a portion of the first turn: its area is one third of the corresponding circular sector whose radius is the final spiral radius. For an accumulated angle $\beta\leq2\pi$,

$$
A(0,\beta)=\frac{a^2\beta^3}{6}
=\frac13\left(\frac12\beta(a\beta)^2\right).
$$

### Proposition 25: the second-turn loop is $7/12$ of the second circle

The region bounded by the second turn and the segment of the initial line joining its endpoints has area

$$
A_2=\frac{7}{12}\pi(2d)^2
=\frac73\pi d^2.
$$

This is the **loop area**, which includes the first-turn region plus the shell between the turns. The shell added by the second turn alone is

$$
R_2=A_2-A_1
=\frac73\pi d^2-\frac13\pi d^2
=2\pi d^2
=6A_1.
$$

The distinction between the loop $A_2$ and the shell $R_2$ is the source of much confusion in popular summaries. Archimedes keeps them separate.

### Proposition 26: the general area of a spiral portion

Consider a forward spiral portion spanning at most one complete revolution. Let its endpoint radii be $r_1,r_2$, with angular span $\Delta\theta$. Its area, bounded by the spiral and the two endpoint radii, is

$$
\boxed{
A=\frac{\Delta\theta}{6}
\left(r_1^2+r_1r_2+r_2^2\right).
}
$$

Equivalently, relative to the circular sector of radius $r_2$ and angle $\Delta\theta$,

$$
\frac{A}{\tfrac12r_2^2\Delta\theta}
=
\frac{r_1^2+r_1r_2+r_2^2}{3r_2^2}.
$$

### Proposition 27: the shells are in arithmetic progression

Let $R_1=A_1$ be the first-turn area, and let $R_n$, for $n\geq2$, be the shell added between the $(n-1)$-st and $n$-th turns. Then

$$
R_2=6R_1,
$$

and

$$
R_3=2R_2,\quad
R_4=3R_2,\quad \ldots,\quad
R_n=(n-1)R_2.
$$

Consequently,

$$
R_1:R_2:R_3:R_4:\cdots
=1:6:12:18:\cdots.
$$

The arithmetic progression applies to the added shells beginning with $R_2$, not to all first-turn and shell areas starting with $R_1$.

### Proposition 28: the two parts of a partial shell

Take a spiral portion with endpoint radii $r_1<r_2$. Draw circles centred at the origin with these radii. Inside the corresponding annular sector, the spiral divides the region into two pieces:

- $L$: between the inner circle and the spiral;
- $U$: between the spiral and the outer circle.

Archimedes gives their ratio, which can be written

$$
\boxed{
\frac{U}{L}
=
\frac{r_1+2r_2}{2r_1+r_2}.
}
$$

Equivalently, putting $h=r_2-r_1$,

$$
\frac{U}{L}
=
\frac{r_1+\tfrac23h}{r_1+\tfrac13h}.
$$

## 6. The two famous constructions

Two consequences are often mentioned alongside the treatise.

### Circle quadrature

Proposition 18 supplies a straight segment equal to a circle's circumference. Together with the circle-area theorem from *Measurement of a Circle*, it enables the construction of an equal-area triangle and hence an equal-area square, once the spiral tangent is available.

The construction is not a straightedge-and-compass construction. It uses the spiral and its tangent, which enlarges the permitted tools.

### Angle trisection

The proportionality of radius and accumulated angle gives a trisection construction. To trisect an angle $\theta$:

1. Locate the point $P$ on the spiral whose ray makes angle $\theta$ with the initial line.
2. Divide the segment $OP$ into three equal parts.
3. Draw the circle centred at $O$ through the one-third point.
4. Where that circle meets the spiral, draw the ray from $O$.

That ray makes angle $\theta/3$.

## 7. Why the spiral matters

The spiral is not merely a curiosity. It is the first curve in the Greek corpus whose tangent and area are both derived from a single kinematic definition. The same parameter $a$ controls both the tangent construction and the area formula.

The results also mark a boundary. The circle cannot be squared with straightedge and compass; the angle cannot be trisected. The spiral does both, but only by adding a new tool. That is the pattern Archimedes repeats elsewhere: the impossible problems become possible when the permitted constructions are enlarged.
