
Asking creative mathematical questions is less about raw inspiration than about applying repeatable problem-posing frameworks — most notably Brown & Walter's "What-If-Not" method, reverse engineering, and the deliberate pushing of a single problem to its extremes. Below are the core techniques, drawn from problem-posing theory and the craft of mathematical problem design.

## The "What-If-Not" Framework

This is the canonical, systematic method for generating new mathematical questions, developed by Stephen Brown and Marion Walter. It works in layers: [matheducators.stackexchange](https://matheducators.stackexchange.com/questions/1380/how-does-one-create-good-math-problems)

1. **Choose a starting point** — any theorem, problem, or mathematical scenario.
2. **List its attributes** — every assumption, constraint, and given condition.
3. **Apply "What-If-Not"** — negate, remove, or alter each attribute one at a time ("What if this weren't a right triangle? What if the field weren't real?").
4. **Pose new questions** — ask what becomes true, false, or interesting under the altered condition.
5. **Analyze** — pursue the most promising resulting question.

You can **cycle** through this repeatedly, producing problems of increasing depth and complexity. The power is that it turns creativity into a checklist: every attribute of a known result is a lever you can pull. [matheducators.stackexchange](https://matheducators.stackexchange.com/questions/1380/how-does-one-create-good-math-problems)

## Reverse Engineering (Answer → Question)

Instead of posing a question to reach an answer, **provide an answer and ask what question could produce it**. For example, give the number 42 and ask: "What problems have this as their solution?" This forces the questioner to explore the space of problems mapping to a given result, often surfishing unexpected connections. It also exposes which constraints are *necessary* versus merely *sufficient*. [nctm](https://www.nctm.org/Conferences-and-Professional-Development/Tips-for-Teachers/Asking-Questions-and-Promoting-Discourse/)

## Push a Single Problem to Its Extremes

Take one problem and exhaust it: ask every possible question about the scenario, alter the given data, tweak assumptions, introduce parameters, and solve it through multiple approaches. Concretely: [reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/math/comments/1fyds9/creating_interesting_math_questions/)

- Vary parameters toward limiting/boundary cases (what happens as n → ∞, as a dimension drops to zero).
- Remove a constraint and ask whether the result still holds, weakens, or breaks.
- Add a constraint and ask what new structure emerges.
- Restate it in a different but equivalent form.

This "extremal" habit is where many research questions originate — a theorem that survives most perturbations but fails at one boundary is often worth a paper.

## Disguise and Repurpose Standard Theorems

A reliable technique for crafting *elegant* problems: start with a neat, widely-accepted mathematical fact, then reframe it in a setting where the underlying truth isn't immediately obvious. Give a different set of data, ask for a different quantity, or place it in an unexpected context. The solver redisCOVERS a known result, but the path is non-obvious. Equally, **repurpose classic problems** by changing their surface — a geometry problem restated as a number-theoretic one, or an algebraic identity hidden inside a combinatorial counting argument. [reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/math/comments/1fyds9/creating_interesting_math_questions/)

## Invert Closed Questions into Open Ones

Replace single-answer questions with ones that admit many valid answers. Instead of "find the area of an 8 × 3 rectangle," ask "how many rectangles have area 24?" The inversion shifts the task from computation to *characterization* — the solver must now describe an entire family, which is structurally closer to how research mathematics actually proceeds. The same move applies at any level: "solve this equation" → "describe all equations with these solution properties." [edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/creating-rich-math-tasks/)

## Generalize and Specialize

Two complementary moves, both rooted in Pólya's "look back" step: [math.libretexts](https://math.libretexts.org/Courses/Coalinga_College/Math_for_Educators_(MATH_010A_and_010B_CID120)/05:_Problem_Solving/5.02:_George_Polya's_Strategy)

- **Specialize** — solve a simpler or more specific version first, then ask what the pattern suggests about the general case.
- **Generalize** — take a specific result and ask under what broader conditions it holds. "Does this extend to higher dimensions? To other rings? To non-commutative settings?"

Each move is a question-generator: every specific result invites a generalization, and every general theorem invites a search for sharpness (is the hypothesis necessary? what's the counterexample when you relax it?).

## Ask Before the Method

Pose the problem *before* teaching or applying the standard technique, inviting intuition first. In a research context this is the analogue of conjecture-before-proof: state the question, let plausible reasoning suggest what *should* be true, and only then seek rigor. Fermi questions — estimation problems emphasizing process and multiple approaches over a single product — serve a similar function of opening up reasoning rather than closing it down. [nctm](https://www.nctm.org/Conferences-and-Professional-Development/Tips-for-Teachers/Asking-Questions-and-Promoting-Discourse/)

## Cross-Domain Translation

Restate a problem in the language of another field. An algebraic identity becomes a geometric construction; a number-theoretic divisibility question becomes a question about group actions; a probabilistic statement becomes a combinatorial one. The translation itself is a creative act, and questions that are trivial in one domain often become deep in another — or reveal that two seemingly unrelated problems are the same problem.

***

The unifying principle across all these techniques is **deliberate perturbation**: take something known and change exactly one thing, then ask what happens. Creativity in mathematical questioning, as Brown and Walter argue, is largely a matter of making the implicit assumptions of a problem explicit — and then denying them. The richest questions tend to come from the attribute you'd least think to question.
