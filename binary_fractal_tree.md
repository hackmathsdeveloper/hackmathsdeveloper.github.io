
The picture is a gallery of **planar binary self-similar fractal trees**. Each tree is generated by repeatedly replacing every terminal branch with two shorter rotated copies, governed by:

\[
(\theta_1,\theta_2,s).
\]

Here \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) are the left/right turning angles from the parent branch direction, and \(s\) is the common child-to-parent length ratio. The rich differences—arched canopies, pentagonal loops, hexagonal lattices, dense fans, and lopsided trees—come entirely from how those three parameters interact.

## Recursive rule

Represent a branch by its starting point \(z\in\mathbb C\), direction \(\phi\), and length \(\ell\). Its endpoint is

\[
z' = z+\ell e^{i\phi}.
\]

At that endpoint, create two child branches:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Left child:}\quad&
(z',\ \phi+\theta_1,\ s\ell),\\[4pt]
\text{Right child:}\quad&
(z',\ \phi-\theta_2,\ s\ell).
\end{aligned}
\]

Starting with one trunk, repeat to depth \(n\). At depth \(n\):

- There are \(2^n\) terminal branches.
- Each branch has length \(s^n\ell_0\).
- The total drawn branch length through depth \(n\) is

\[
L_n
=
\ell_0\sum_{k=0}^{n}(2s)^k
=
\begin{cases}
\ell_0\dfrac{1-(2s)^{n+1}}{1-2s}, & s\ne \frac12,\\[8pt]
(n+1)\ell_0, & s=\frac12.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus, the total idealized line length stays finite only when

\[
2s<1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
s<\frac12.
\]

Most images use \(s>1/2\), so the theoretical total branch length diverges as recursion continues—even though the drawing can remain inside a bounded canopy because directions continually turn and cancel spatially.

This is the standard recursive definition of a binary fractal tree: branches shrink by a fixed factor and bifurcate indefinitely. [dalspace.library.dal](https://dalspace.library.dal.ca/items/afae8734-c054-468a-9393-41de5ad742f6)

## Complex-number formulation

It is especially natural to encode the construction using complex similarities. Normalize the trunk to run from \(0\) to \(1\). The two branch transformations are

\[
F_L(z)=1+s e^{i\theta_1}z,
\qquad
F_R(z)=1+s e^{-i\theta_2}z.
\]

The limiting canopy \(K\) is the compact set satisfying the iterated-function-system equation

\[
K=F_L(K)\cup F_R(K).
\]

Equivalently, every infinite left/right decision sequence

\[
\omega=\omega_1\omega_2\omega_3\cdots,
\qquad \omega_j\in\{L,R\},
\]

specifies a limiting tip. Let

\[
a_L=s e^{i\theta_1},
\qquad
a_R=s e^{-i\theta_2}.
\]

Then the endpoint corresponding to the word \(\omega\) is

\[
z_\omega
=
1+a_{\omega_1}
+a_{\omega_1}a_{\omega_2}
+a_{\omega_1}a_{\omega_2}a_{\omega_3}
+\cdots.
\]

Since \(|a_L|=|a_R|=s<1\), this series converges.

That formula makes the self-similarity exact: after taking a left branch, everything above it is a copy of the entire tree, scaled by \(s\) and rotated by \(\theta_1\); after taking a right branch, it is another scaled copy, rotated by \(-\theta_2\).

## Geometric regimes

The visual type is controlled by two mostly independent aspects:

| Feature | Controlled mainly by | Effect |
|---|---:|---|
| Overall scale and density | \(s\) | Larger \(s\) makes descendants longer, canopies denser, and overlaps more likely |
| Mirror symmetry | Whether \(\theta_1=\theta_2\) | Equal angles give a reflection-symmetric tree |
| Directional bias | \(\theta_1-\theta_2\) | Unequal angles produce a leaning or one-sided canopy |
| Angular resonance | Rational relations between angles and \(360^\circ\) | Repeated directions can close into polygons or lattices |
| Overlap/contact | Both angles and \(s\) | Exact parameter values can make tips touch, branches overlap, or subtrees meet |

### 1. Symmetric trees

If

\[
\theta_1=\theta_2=\theta,
\]

then the tree is invariant under reflection across the trunk’s axis. The fourth image,

\[
\theta_1=\theta_2=18^\circ,
\qquad
s\approx0.618034,
\]

is a symmetric example.

The number

\[
0.618034\ldots=\frac1\varphi=\varphi-1,
\qquad
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},
\]

is the reciprocal of the golden ratio. This kind of value occurs in “self-contacting” tree constructions, where geometry is tuned so branches or tips meet without arbitrary crossings. Golden-ratio-scaled fractal trees are a documented family, including asymmetric variants with \(36^\circ\) rotations. [blog.wolfram](https://blog.wolfram.com/2014/05/22/adventures-into-the-mathematical-forest-of-fractal-trees/)

### 2. Asymmetric trees

If

\[
\theta_1\ne\theta_2,
\]

the left and right maps are no longer mirror images. The canopy can curve or accumulate on one side, as in examples such as

\[
(\theta_1,\theta_2,s)=(36^\circ,15^\circ,0.66)
\]

or

\[
(15^\circ,30^\circ,0.72).
\]

The asymmetry compounds down the tree. A path containing \(k\) left turns and \(n-k\) right turns has total direction

\[
\phi
=
k\theta_1-(n-k)\theta_2,
\]

up to the starting orientation. If the two turns do not balance statistically or arithmetically, one family of branch words increasingly favors one side of the plane.

A useful generalization is to give the two sides *different* scale factors:

\[
F_L(z)=1+s_L e^{i\theta_1}z,
\qquad
F_R(z)=1+s_R e^{-i\theta_2}z.
\]

Then one side can be structurally dominant even if \(\theta_1=\theta_2\). This models unequal biological branching, unequal capital allocation in a decision tree, or biased search trees.

## Dimension and density

If both child maps use the same contraction \(s\) and their copies do not overlap too much, the similarity dimension \(D\) satisfies

\[
2s^D=1.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
D=\frac{\log 2}{\log(1/s)}
=
-\frac{\log 2}{\log s}.
}
\]

This is the same scaling relation used for self-similar constructions: \(N\) copies scaled by a ratio \(r\) have similarity dimension determined by \(N r^D=1\). [lsa.umich](https://lsa.umich.edu/content/dam/math-assets/math-document/reu-documents/ugradreu/2020/Gao,Jingyi.pdf)

For several values in the figure:

| Scale \(s\) | Similarity dimension \(D=\log 2/\log(1/s)\) | Interpretation |
|---:|---:|---|
| \(0.55\) | \(1.159\) | Sparse, tree-like boundary |
| \(0.618034\approx 1/\varphi\) | \(1.440\) | Intermediate, often associated with geometric contact patterns |
| \(0.66\) | \(1.670\) | Denser branching, more likely to approach a filled canopy |
| \(0.70\) | \(1.943\) | Nearly plane-filling in the no-overlap idealization |
| \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.707107\) | \(2\) | Critical planar value |
| \(0.72\) | \(2.114\) | The naive similarity dimension exceeds 2, so planar overlaps must occur |
| \(0.80\) | \(3.106\) | Strong overlap is unavoidable in \(\mathbb R^2\) |

The number \(D\) above is the **similarity dimension**. When the two scaled copies overlap, the actual Hausdorff dimension of the planar limit set can be lower; it can never exceed \(2\). The source discussing fractal trees makes the same distinction: the canopy can have a nontrivial fractal dimension, while the finite-width or line-segment branch skeleton itself remains one-dimensional. [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960077908005080)

The critical value is especially important:

\[
2s^2=1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
s=\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]

- If \(s<1/\sqrt2\), then \(D<2\): an open, sparse fractal canopy is plausible.
- If \(s=1/\sqrt2\), then \(D=2\): the construction sits at the plane-filling threshold.
- If \(s>1/\sqrt2\), the formal dimension is \(>2\), impossible for a planar non-overlapping set; overlaps, contact, or thick regions must appear.

That explains why the examples at \(s=0.8, 0.9,\) and \(0.92\) appear as dense fans or heavily interwoven regions.

## Why polygons appear

The lower-left examples with \(60^\circ\) angles visibly generate hexagonal motifs. This occurs because rotations by \(60^\circ\) lie in the finite cyclic rotation group

\[
C_6=\left\{e^{2\pi i k/6}:k=0,\dots,5\right\}.
\]

Repeated left/right turns can only point in six principal directions. Therefore, branch paths naturally trace or nearly trace hexagonal grids.

More generally, if the branch angles are rational multiples of a full turn:

\[
\theta_1,\theta_2\in 360^\circ\cdot\mathbb Q,
\]

then only finitely many orientations occur. This tends to generate polygonal or lattice-like motifs.

Examples:

| Angles | Orientation structure | Typical visual effect |
|---|---|---|
| \(60^\circ,60^\circ\) | Six directions | Hexagons, honeycombs, triangular lattice features |
| \(45^\circ,45^\circ\) | Eight directions | Square and octagonal motifs |
| \(36^\circ,36^\circ\) | Ten directions | Pentagonal or decagonal structure |
| \(72^\circ,72^\circ\) | Five directions | Pentagonal star-like patterns |
| Irrational multiples of \(180^\circ\) | Infinitely many directions | Less periodic, more cloud-like canopies |

For \(\theta_1=\theta_2=60^\circ\), a turn sequence can produce a net \(360^\circ\) rotation after six same-direction choices. This angular closure is why hexagonal substructures recur. The scale \(s\) determines whether these directional cycles merely suggest polygons, meet exactly, or overlap.

## Contact equations

The most interesting parameter values arise when two different branch words reach the same point. Let \(u\) and \(v\) be two finite words in \(\{L,R\}\). A **contact condition** is

\[
z_u=z_v.
\]

Since each endpoint is a finite sum of products of \(a_L\) and \(a_R\), this is an algebraic equation in

\[
s,\quad e^{i\theta_1},\quad e^{-i\theta_2}.
\]

For a symmetric tree, write

\[
a=s e^{i\theta},
\qquad
\bar a=s e^{-i\theta}.
\]

Then contact relations take the form

\[
P(a,\bar a)=0,
\]

where \(P\) is a polynomial with integer coefficients derived from two distinct words. Splitting into real and imaginary parts gives equations in

\[
s,\qquad \cos\theta,\qquad \sin\theta.
\]

This is how “special” scales such as \(1/\varphi\) arise: they are not decorative numerical choices; they solve geometric closure or tip-contact equations. The literature on self-contacting binary fractal trees specifically studies cases where left and right subtrees intersect at tips. [math.union](https://www.math.union.edu/research/fractaltrees/)

A simple practical classification is:

- **Separated:** descendant copies remain disjoint.
- **Self-contacting:** copies touch at isolated endpoints.
- **Overlapping:** copies share arcs or regions, producing denser, more complicated geometry.
- **Lattice-resonant:** finite direction sets make repeated polygonal cells.
- **Quasi-periodic/nonresonant:** angles generate many orientations, giving irregular foliated canopies.

## Stronger generalizations

The pictured model uses one scale \(s\), two fixed angles, and exactly two children. It is only the first member of a broad family.

### Variable left/right scales

\[
F_L(z)=1+s_L e^{i\theta_L}z,
\qquad
F_R(z)=1+s_R e^{-i\theta_R}z.
\]

Its similarity dimension, when the open-set/no-overlap assumptions are suitable, is defined implicitly by Moran’s equation:

\[
\boxed{
s_L^D+s_R^D=1.
}
\]

For \(s_L=s_R=s\), this reduces to \(2s^D=1\). Moran’s equation is the standard dimension relation for unequal contraction ratios. [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960077908005080)

### \(m\)-ary trees

Instead of two children, use \(m\) child transformations:

\[
F_j(z)=1+s_j e^{i\theta_j}z,
\qquad
j=1,\ldots,m.
\]

Then

\[
K=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m} F_j(K),
\]

and the corresponding similarity dimension is determined by

\[
\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_j^D=1.
\]

If all children have equal scale \(s\),

\[
m s^D=1,
\qquad
D=\frac{\log m}{\log(1/s)}.
\]

This lets you generate ternary coral-like trees, \(5\)-fold flower canopies, or radial \(n\)-ary branching systems.

### Depth-dependent parameters

Let parameters depend on the generation \(n\):

\[
(\theta_{1,n},\theta_{2,n},s_n).
\]

For example,

\[
s_n=s_0\lambda^n,
\]

makes branches shrink faster with depth, while

\[
\theta_{1,n}=\theta+\varepsilon\sin(n\alpha)
\]

creates a slowly oscillating or wind-swept canopy. If \(\alpha/\pi\) is irrational, the angular modulation does not repeat periodically.

### Stochastic trees

Choose the branch angles and scales randomly:

\[
\theta_L\sim \mathcal D_L,
\qquad
\theta_R\sim \mathcal D_R,
\qquad
s_L,s_R\sim\mathcal S.
\]

This produces a more botanical model. You can additionally make branch continuation probabilistic:

\[
\Pr(\text{branch continues at depth }n)=p_n.
\]

If \(p_n=p\) is constant, the expected number of branches at generation \(n\) becomes

\[
(2p)^n,
\]

rather than \(2^n\). This is useful for simulating sparse trees, dendrites, coral, bronchial structures, or procedural vegetation.

### Thickness and conservation laws

Attach a width \(w\) to each branch. If the parent has width \(w\), choose child widths via a conservation-style rule

\[
w^\alpha=w_L^\alpha+w_R^\alpha.
\]

For equal child widths,

\[
w_L=w_R=2^{-1/\alpha}w.
\]

Typical choices include:

- \(\alpha=2\): conservation of cross-sectional area
- \(\alpha=3\): a common idealized allometric rule for vascular branching
- Arbitrary \(\alpha\): an aesthetic or structural parameter

Such parent–child power-law constraints are used in bifurcating-tree modeling. [lsa.umich](https://lsa.umich.edu/content/dam/math-assets/math-document/reu-documents/ugradreu/2020/Gao,Jingyi.pdf)

## A compact programmable model

In pseudocode:

```text
drawBranch(position z, angle φ, length ℓ, depth n):
    endpoint = z + ℓ · (cos φ, sin φ)
    drawLine(z, endpoint)

    if n == 0:
        return

    drawBranch(endpoint, φ + θ1, sL · ℓ, n - 1)
    drawBranch(endpoint, φ - θ2, sR · ℓ, n - 1)
```

For the exact family in the image, set

```text
sL = sR = s
```

and vary \(\theta_1,\theta_2,s\).

A useful parameter sweep would be:

\[
\theta_1,\theta_2\in[0^\circ,90^\circ],
\qquad
s\in[0.45,0.90].
\]

Color each parameter triple according to a geometric statistic:

- number of segment intersections,
- number of near tip-contacts,
- area of the convex hull,
- estimated box-counting dimension,
- entropy of branch-direction distribution,
- left/right imbalance of the canopy.

That turns the image family into a genuine parameter-space study rather than a collection of isolated designs.

## Central interpretation

The pattern is best understood as an **iterated function system on a binary symbolic tree**:

\[
\{L,R\}^{\mathbb N}
\longrightarrow
\mathbb C.
\]

Every infinite binary word describes a limiting tip; geometry is determined by the two complex contractions

\[
s e^{i\theta_1},
\qquad
s e^{-i\theta_2}.
\]

The angles define the group of available orientations, while the contraction factor determines how strongly descendant copies overlap. Rational angle relations create lattice and polygon effects; special algebraic values of \(s\) can force exact self-contact; and \(s=1/\sqrt2\) marks the formal dimension-\(2\) threshold for equal-scale binary branching.
