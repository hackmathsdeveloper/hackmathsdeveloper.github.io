## My recommendation for you

Given your interest in proof-oriented mathematics and deeper subjects, use this sequence:

1. **Visual Group Theory**  
   Spend enough time to make the core objects feel natural:
   - Dihedral groups \(D_n\)
   - Permutation groups \(S_n\)
   - Cyclic groups and generators
   - Group actions as symmetries
   - Cayley graphs and quotient intuition

2. **Daniel Chan’s Dummit-and-Foote-based course**  
   Make this your main proof course. Work problems after every topic rather than binge-watching lectures. [youtube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D_8XHgqZDVo)

3. **The Gray Cuber’s small-order classification videos**  
   Use these whenever the abstraction feels detached. Classifying groups of orders \(1,2,3,4,6,8,\dots\) forces you to apply orbit-stabilizer, Sylow ideas, normality, semidirect products, and presentations in a concrete setting. [youtube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tGCqP2ytP14)

4. **A textbook problem source in parallel**  
   Video alone will not build proof fluency. Pair the series with one of:
   - *Contemporary Abstract Algebra* — Joseph Gallian: most approachable, many examples and exercises.
   - *Abstract Algebra* — Dummit & Foote: comprehensive reference; excellent if you expect to continue into rings, fields, Galois theory, representation theory, or algebraic number theory.
   - *Algebra* — Michael Artin: elegant conceptual route, especially strong on linear algebra, actions, and geometry connections.
   - *A Book of Abstract Algebra* — Charles Pinter: gentler, proof-building oriented start.

## A practical viewing syllabus

A solid first-course sequence is:

1. Definitions, examples, and basic proof techniques  
2. Subgroups, generated subgroups, cyclic groups  
3. Lagrange’s theorem and element orders  
4. Cosets and normal subgroups  
5. Homomorphisms, kernels, images, and isomorphism theorems  
6. Permutation groups and Cayley’s theorem  
7. Direct products and finitely generated abelian groups  
8. Group actions, orbits, stabilizers, class equation  
9. Sylow theorems and classifications of small finite groups  
10. Optional next step: semidirect products, group presentations, solvable/nilpotent groups, representations

A useful rule: after each lecture, prove at least two results independently and solve 5–10 exercises. For example, after learning cosets, prove that a subgroup \(N\triangleleft G\) is normal exactly when its left and right cosets agree, then compute quotient groups such as \(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z \,/\, \langle 4\rangle\).

## What to avoid

- Do not use “visual” material as your whole course: it builds insight but rarely provides enough sustained proof practice.
- Do not begin with representation theory, Lie groups, or physics group theory if your finite-group foundation is incomplete.
- Do not watch all lectures before doing exercises. Group theory becomes clear when you manipulate examples and write proofs.

The most balanced path is **Tom Crawford for intuition + Daniel Chan for rigorous development + Gallian or Dummit & Foote exercises**. This gives you both conceptual feel and the proof machinery needed for later algebra, topology, number theory, and mathematical physics. [youtube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D_8XHgqZDVo)
