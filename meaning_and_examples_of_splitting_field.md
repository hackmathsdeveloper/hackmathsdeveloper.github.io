
A **splitting field** is the smallest extension of a base field that contains every root of a given polynomial, so that the polynomial factors completely into linear factors. Equivalently, if \(f(x)\in F[x]\) has roots \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\), then its splitting field over \(F\) is

\[
F(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n),
\]

the field obtained by adjoining all of its roots to \(F\). In that field,

\[
f(x)=a\prod_{i=1}^n(x-\alpha_i)
\]

for some \(a\in F\), with repeated roots included according to multiplicity. This “generated by its roots” condition is what makes the field minimal; a bigger field in which \(f\) also splits need not itself be the splitting field. [math.libretexts](https://math.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Abstract_and_Geometric_Algebra/Abstract_Algebra:_Theory_and_Applications_(Judson)/21:_Fields/21.02:_Splitting_Fields)

## Why it matters

Splitting fields identify exactly how much algebraic structure must be added to a field to reveal all roots of a polynomial. They are central to **Galois theory**:

- They turn a polynomial’s roots into elements of one common field.
- Automorphisms of the splitting field can permute those roots.
- For separable polynomials, the splitting field is a finite normal, separable extension—hence a finite Galois extension.
- Its degree and automorphism group encode facts about solvability by radicals and symmetries of the roots.

For example, \(x^3-2\) has one real root, \(\sqrt [math.ucdavis](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~egorskiy/MAT150C-s17/Galois.pdf){2}\), but adjoining that root alone does *not* give all roots. The two non-real roots require a primitive cube root of unity \(\omega\), so the splitting field is \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.ucdavis](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~egorskiy/MAT150C-s17/Galois.pdf){2},\omega)\), not merely \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.ucdavis](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~egorskiy/MAT150C-s17/Galois.pdf){2})\).

## Definition carefully stated

Let \(f(x)\in F[x]\). A field extension \(E/F\) is a splitting field of \(f\) over \(F\) when:

1. \(f\) **splits completely** over \(E\), meaning it is a product of linear factors in \(E[x]\).
2. \(E\) is generated over \(F\) by the roots of \(f\):
   \[
   E=F(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n).
   \]

The definition is often phrased as “the minimal field extension of \(F\) in which \(f\) splits.” The two formulations agree. [dummit.cos.northeastern](https://dummit.cos.northeastern.edu/teaching_fa20_5111/5111_lecture_08_splitting_fields_algebraic_closures.pdf)

A useful warning: “minimal” does **not** mean smallest by set inclusion among every possible ambient field, since roots can live in differently embedded copies of a field. It means unique **up to an \(F\)-isomorphism**: any two splitting fields of the same polynomial over \(F\) are isomorphic in a way that fixes \(F\).

| 2 | \(x^2+1\in\mathbb Q[x]\) | \((x-i)(x+i)\) | \(\mathbb Q(i)\) |
| 3 | \(x^2-5x+6\in\mathbb Q[x]\) | \((x-2)(x-3)\) | \(\mathbb Q\) |

| 5 | \(x^3-1\in\mathbb Q[x]\) | \((x-1)(x-\omega)(x-\omega^2)\) | \(\mathbb Q(\omega)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})\) |
| 6 | \(x^4-2\in\mathbb Q[x]\) | \(\prod_{k=0}^{3}(x-i^k\sqrt [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/SplittingField.html)2)\) | \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/SplittingField.html)2,i)\) |
| 7 | \(x^4+1\in\mathbb Q[x]\) | Roots are primitive eighth roots of unity | \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)=\mathbb Q(i,\sqrt2)\) |

| 9 | \(x^3-3x+1\in\mathbb Q[x]\) | All three roots are real, but generally not rational | \(\mathbb Q(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)\) |

Here \(\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}\) denotes a primitive \(n\)-th root of unity.

## Worked explanations

### 1. \(x^2-2\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

The roots are \(+\sqrt2\) and \(-\sqrt2\). Once \(\sqrt2\) is adjoined, its negative is automatically present:

\[
x^2-2=(x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2).
\]

Thus,

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^2-2)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
\]

This is a degree-2 extension of \(\mathbb Q\). It is the standard quadratic splitting-field example. [math.utoronto](https://www.math.utoronto.ca/gscott/nov2_2015.pdf)

### 2. \(x^2+1\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

The roots are \(i\) and \(-i\), neither of which is rational:

\[
x^2+1=(x-i)(x+i).
\]

Adjoining \(i\) gives both roots, hence

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^2+1)=\mathbb Q(i).
\]

Although the polynomial also splits in \(\mathbb C\), \(\mathbb C\) is not its splitting field over \(\mathbb Q\), because \(\mathbb Q(i)\) is a proper smaller field where it already splits. [math.ucsd](https://www.math.ucsd.edu/~jmckerna/Teaching/16-17/Winter/200B/l_8.pdf)

### 3. \(x^2-5x+6\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

This factors over the original field:

\[
x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3).
\]

Both roots already lie in \(\mathbb Q\). No extension is necessary:

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^2-5x+6)=\mathbb Q.
\]

A splitting field can therefore be the base field itself. [math.ucsd](https://www.math.ucsd.edu/~jmckerna/Teaching/16-17/Winter/200B/l_8.pdf)

### 4. \(x^3-2\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

Let \(\alpha=\sqrt [math.ucdavis](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~egorskiy/MAT150C-s17/Galois.pdf){2}\). The roots are

\[
\alpha,\qquad \omega\alpha,\qquad \omega^2\alpha,
\]

where

\[
\omega=\frac{-1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}, \qquad \omega^3=1,\quad \omega\ne1.
\]

So,

\[
x^3-2=(x-\alpha)(x-\omega\alpha)(x-\omega^2\alpha).
\]

Adjoining \(\alpha\) produces the real root only; \(\omega\) must also be included for the two complex roots. Therefore,

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^3-2)
=\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.ucdavis](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~egorskiy/MAT150C-s17/Galois.pdf)2,\omega).
\]

This is a classic example where adjoining one root does not suffice. Its splitting field has degree \(6\) over \(\mathbb Q\).

### 5. \(x^3-1\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

We have

\[
x^3-1=(x-1)(x^2+x+1).
\]

The quadratic factor has roots \(\omega,\omega^2\), so

\[
x^3-1=(x-1)(x-\omega)(x-\omega^2).
\]

Since \(1\in\mathbb Q\), the only new ingredient is \(\omega\). Hence

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^3-1)
=\mathbb Q(\omega)
=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3}).
\]

### 6. \(x^4-2\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

Let \(\alpha=\sqrt [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/SplittingField.html){2}\). The roots are

\[
\alpha,\quad i\alpha,\quad -\alpha,\quad -i\alpha.
\]

Thus,

\[
x^4-2=(x-\alpha)(x+\alpha)(x-i\alpha)(x+i\alpha).
\]

We need both \(\sqrt [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/SplittingField.html){2}\) and \(i\), giving

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^4-2)
=\mathbb Q(\sqrt [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/SplittingField.html){2},i).
\]

This example demonstrates that even a polynomial with a real radical root can require a complex root of unity to capture all roots.

### 7. \(x^4+1\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

The equation \(x^4=-1\) has roots

\[
e^{i\pi/4},\ e^{3i\pi/4},\ e^{5i\pi/4},\ e^{7i\pi/4}.
\]

Equivalently, they are the primitive eighth roots of unity:

\[
\zeta_8,\ \zeta_8^3,\ \zeta_8^5,\ \zeta_8^7.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^4+1)=\mathbb Q(\zeta_8).
\]

Since

\[
\zeta_8=\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2},
\]

one may write the same field as

\[
\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)=\mathbb Q(i,\sqrt2).
\]

### 8. \(x^5-1\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

Every root is a fifth root of unity:

\[
1,\zeta_5,\zeta_5^2,\zeta_5^3,\zeta_5^4.
\]

Hence the splitting field is the fifth cyclotomic field:

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^5-1)=\mathbb Q(\zeta_5).
\]

It has degree

\[
[\mathbb Q(\zeta_5):\mathbb Q]=\varphi(5)=4,
\]

where \(\varphi\) is Euler’s totient function. More generally,

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^n-1)=\mathbb Q(\zeta_n),
\]

the \(n\)-th cyclotomic field.

### 9. \(x^3-3x+1\) over \(\mathbb Q\)

The rational-root test shows that this polynomial has no rational root, so it is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\). Its discriminant is

\[
\Delta=-4(-3)^3-27(1)^2=81=9^2.
\]

A cubic with square discriminant has Galois group contained in \(A_3\); here the polynomial has three real roots. If \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3\) are those roots, the splitting field is

\[
\mathbb Q(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3).
\]

In fact, for this particular irreducible cubic, adjoining one root already yields the full splitting field:

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb Q}(x^3-3x+1)=\mathbb Q(\alpha_1),
\]

and the extension has degree \(3\). This contrasts sharply with \(x^3-2\), where one root generates only a degree-3 subfield of the degree-6 splitting field.

### 10. \(x^2+1\) over \(\mathbb R\)

Changing the base field changes the splitting field. Over \(\mathbb R\),

\[
x^2+1=(x-i)(x+i),
\]

but \(i\notin\mathbb R\). Adjoining \(i\) to \(\mathbb R\) yields all complex numbers:

\[
\mathbb R(i)=\mathbb C.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\operatorname{Split}_{\mathbb R}(x^2+1)=\mathbb C.
\]

The same polynomial had splitting field \(\mathbb Q(i)\) over \(\mathbb Q\), illustrating that a splitting field is always specified **over a chosen base field**. [math.ucdavis](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~egorskiy/MAT150C-s17/Galois.pdf)

## Practical method

To find the splitting field of \(f(x)\in F[x]\):

1. Factor \(f\) as far as possible over \(F\).
2. Find one root of an irreducible factor and adjoin it to form a larger field.
3. Factor the remaining polynomial over that new field.
4. Continue adjoining any missing roots until all irreducible factors are linear.
5. Express the result as \(F(\text{all roots})\).

For polynomials of the form \(x^n-a\), this often means adjoining:

\[
\sqrt[n]{a}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\zeta_n,
\]

because the roots are

\[
\sqrt[n]{a},\ \zeta_n\sqrt[n]{a},\ \zeta_n^2\sqrt[n]{a},\ldots,\zeta_n^{n-1}\sqrt[n]{a}.
\]

The field generated by the roots is precisely the desired splitting field. [math.ucsd](https://www.math.ucsd.edu/~jmckerna/Teaching/16-17/Winter/200B/l_8.pdf)
