
"On the Equilibrium of Planes" (often referred to as *De Planis Aequilibriis*) is one of Archimedes' foundational works in mechanics and mathematics. It is divided into two books. Below is a comprehensive list of the results (propositions) derived in the work, based on the standard Greek text (ed. Heiberg) and the critical edition by E.J. Dijksterhuis (*Archimedes*, 1956) and the translation by T.L. Heath (*The Works of Archimedes*, 1897).

---

## Book I

Book I establishes the fundamental principles of the lever and the concept of the center of gravity, then applies them to find the centers of gravity of various plane figures.

### Preliminary Postulates and Definitions
- **Postulate 1:** Equal weights at equal distances from the fulcrum are in equilibrium.
- **Postulate 2:** Equal weights at unequal distances are not in equilibrium; the weight at the greater distance inclines downward.
- **Postulate 3:** If two equal weights are in equilibrium at certain distances, and unequal weights are added to them, equilibrium is not maintained.
- **Postulate 4:** The center of gravity of a homogeneous body is a point such that, if the body is suspended from it, it remains at rest in any position.
- **Postulate 5:** If a magnitude is suspended from a point, its center of gravity lies on the vertical line through the point of suspension.
- **Postulate 6:** If two magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, their combined center of gravity lies at the fulcrum.
- **Definition:** The center of gravity of a plane figure is the point about which the figure balances.

### Propositions

**Proposition 1:**  
If two equal magnitudes have their centers of gravity at equal distances from a point on the line joining them, that point is the center of gravity of the combined magnitude.

**Proposition 2:**  
If two unequal magnitudes have their centers of gravity on a line, and the distances from a point on that line are inversely proportional to the magnitudes, then that point is the center of gravity of the combined magnitude. (The fundamental law of the lever.)

**Proposition 3:**  
If two unequal magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, their combined center of gravity is at the fulcrum. (Converse of Prop. 2.)

**Proposition 4:**  
If two equal magnitudes have their centers of gravity at unequal distances from a point, the center of gravity of the combined magnitude lies on the side of the greater distance.

**Proposition 5:**  
If three magnitudes have their centers of gravity on a straight line, and the center of gravity of the whole is known, then the center of gravity of any one of them can be determined from the others.

**Proposition 6:**  
If two magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, and a third magnitude is added to one of them, the equilibrium is disturbed.

**Proposition 7:**  
If two magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, and equal magnitudes are added to both, equilibrium is preserved.

**Proposition 8:**  
If two magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, and unequal magnitudes are added to both, equilibrium is not preserved.

**Proposition 9:**  
If two magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, and magnitudes are subtracted from both in the same ratio, equilibrium is preserved.

**Proposition 10:**  
If two magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, and magnitudes are subtracted from both in unequal ratios, equilibrium is not preserved.

**Proposition 11:**  
If two magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, and the center of gravity of one is moved, the equilibrium is disturbed.

**Proposition 12:**  
If two magnitudes are in equilibrium at distances inversely proportional to their weights, and the center of gravity of the combined magnitude is found, it lies at the fulcrum.

**Proposition 13:**  
The center of gravity of a triangle lies on the median line (the line from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side).

**Proposition 14:**  
The center of gravity of a triangle is the point where the three medians intersect, and it divides each median in the ratio 2:1 (from vertex to midpoint).

**Proposition 15:**  
The center of gravity of a parallelogram is the intersection of its diagonals.

**Proposition 16:**  
The center of gravity of a trapezoid with parallel sides lies on the line joining the midpoints of the parallel sides.

**Proposition 17:**  
The center of gravity of a trapezoid divides the line joining the midpoints of the parallel sides in a ratio determined by the lengths of the parallel sides. (Specifically, if the parallel sides are \(a\) and \(b\), the center of gravity is at a distance from the midpoint of side \(a\) proportional to \(a + 2b\) over \(a + b\) times the height, or equivalently, the segment lengths are in the ratio \((2a+b):(a+2b)\).)

**Proposition 18:**  
The center of gravity of a circle is its center.

**Proposition 19:**  
The center of gravity of a semicircle lies on the radius perpendicular to the diameter, at a distance from the center equal to \(4r/3\pi\) (where \(r\) is the radius). (Archimedes states this geometrically.)

**Proposition 20:**  
The center of gravity of a segment of a circle (a circular segment bounded by a chord and an arc) lies on the axis of symmetry, at a distance from the center determined by the geometry of the segment. (Archimedes gives a construction.)

**Proposition 21:**  
The center of gravity of a sector of a circle lies on the axis of symmetry, at a distance from the center equal to \(2r \sin(\alpha/2) / (3\alpha/2)\) for a sector of angle \(\alpha\). (Archimedes states this geometrically.)

**Proposition 22:**  
The center of gravity of a parabolic segment lies on the axis of symmetry, at a distance from the vertex equal to \(3/5\) of the height of the segment. (This is a key result.)

**Proposition 23:**  
The center of gravity of a parabolic segment is at a distance from the base equal to \(2/5\) of the height (equivalently, \(3/5\) from the vertex).

**Proposition 24:**  
The center of gravity of a parabolic area bounded by two parabolas (or a parabolic segment and its complement) is determined by a specific construction.

## Book II

Book II is shorter and deals with the equilibrium of a parabolic segment when suspended from a point, and related problems. It contains 10 propositions.

**Proposition 1:**  
If a parabolic segment is suspended from a point on its axis, it will hang with its axis vertical. (The center of gravity lies on the axis.)

**Proposition 2:**  
If a parabolic segment is suspended from a point on its base (the chord), the axis will not be vertical unless the point is the midpoint of the base. The segment will rotate until the center of gravity is vertically below the point of suspension.

**Proposition 3:**  
Given a parabolic segment suspended from a point on its base, the angle the axis makes with the vertical is determined by the position of the suspension point.

**Proposition 4:**  
If a parabolic segment is suspended from a point on its curve, the equilibrium position is determined by the condition that the center of gravity lies vertically below the suspension point.

**Proposition 5:**  
The center of gravity of a parabolic segment is used to determine the equilibrium position when the segment is suspended from various points.

**Proposition 6:**  
If a parabolic segment is suspended from a point on its base, the axis makes an angle with the vertical such that the tangent of the angle is related to the ratio of the distances from the suspension point to the ends of the base.

**Proposition 7:**  
A construction is given for finding the equilibrium position of a parabolic segment suspended from a given point.

**Proposition 8:**  
If a parabolic segment is suspended from a point on its curve, the equilibrium position is found by a geometric construction involving the center of gravity.

**Proposition 9:**  
The equilibrium of a parabolic segment suspended from a point on its base is analyzed in terms of the ratio of the segments of the base.

**Proposition 10:**  
A general construction is given for determining the equilibrium position of a parabolic segment suspended from any point on its perimeter.

## Summary of Key Results

| Result | Statement |
|--------|-----------|
| Law of the lever | Two weights balance at distances inversely proportional to their weights (Props. I.2–3). |
| Center of gravity of a triangle | Intersection of medians, dividing each median 2:1 (Props. I.13–14). |
| Center of gravity of a parallelogram | Intersection of diagonals (Prop. I.15). |
| Center of gravity of a trapezoid | On the line joining midpoints of parallel sides, at a ratio determined by the side lengths (Props. I.16–17). |
| Center of gravity of a circle | The center (Prop. I.18). |
| Center of gravity of a semicircle | On the perpendicular radius, at \(4r/3\pi\) from the center (Prop. I.19). |
| Center of gravity of a circular segment | On the axis of symmetry, determined geometrically (Prop. I.20). |
| Center of gravity of a circular sector | On the axis of symmetry, at \(2r\sin(\alpha/2)/(3\alpha/2)\) from the center (Prop. I.21). |
| Center of gravity of a parabolic segment | On the axis, \(3/5\) of the height from the vertex (or \(2/5\) from the base) (Props. I.22–23). |
| Equilibrium of suspended parabolic segment | Conditions for equilibrium when suspended from points on axis, base, or curve (Book II, Props. 1–10). |

---

These results form the foundation of statics and the theory of centers of gravity in ancient mathematics, and they represent one of Archimedes' most influential contributions to mechanics and geometry.
