
## Short answer

The **Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture** (BSD) is a conjecture saying that the number of rational solutions of an elliptic curve is encoded by the behavior of its associated analytic object, the **\(L\)-function**, near \(s=1\).

It is **not proven in general**. It is one of the **Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems**, with a \$1 million prize. Some important special cases are known, especially when the analytic rank is \(0\) or \(1\), but the full conjecture remains open.

---

## 1. The basic setting: elliptic curves and rational points

Take an elliptic curve over \(\mathbb{Q}\), for example

\[
E: y^2+y=x^3-x.
\]

A central question is:

> How many rational points \((x,y)\in \mathbb{Q}^2\) satisfy the equation?

The **Mordell–Weil theorem** says that the set of rational points forms a finitely generated abelian group:

\[
E(\mathbb{Q}) \cong E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}} \oplus \mathbb{Z}^r.
\]

Here:

- \(E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}\) is the finite torsion subgroup,
- \(r\) is the **rank** of the elliptic curve.

The rank measures the number of independent rational points of infinite order.

- If \(r=0\), then \(E(\mathbb{Q})\) is finite.
- If \(r\ge 1\), then \(E(\mathbb{Q})\) is infinite.
- If \(r=1\), then all rational points are generated by one point, up to torsion.

For the curve

\[
y^2+y=x^3-x,
\]

one has

\[
E(\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z},
\]

so the rank is \(1\), generated by \(P=(0,0)\).

---

## 2. The \(L\)-function of an elliptic curve

To every elliptic curve \(E/\mathbb{Q}\), one associates an analytic object called its **Hasse–Weil \(L\)-function**.

For primes \(p\) of good reduction, define

\[
a_p = p+1-\#E(\mathbb{F}_p),
\]

where \(\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) is the number of points on the reduced curve modulo \(p\).

Then the \(L\)-function is

\[
L(E,s)
=
\prod_{p \text{ good}}
\left(1-a_pp^{-s}+p^{1-2s}\right)^{-1}
\cdot
\prod_{p \text{ bad}}
(\text{local factor})^{-1}.
\]

This is initially defined for \(\operatorname{Re}(s)>3/2\), but by modularity it extends to an entire function on \(\mathbb{C}\) and satisfies a functional equation.

The important point is that \(L(E,s)\) is an analytic object, while the rational points on \(E\) are algebraic/arithmetic objects.

BSD predicts a deep bridge between them.

---

## 3. The weak BSD conjecture

Define the **analytic rank** of \(E\) by

\[
r_{\mathrm{an}}(E)
=
\operatorname{ord}_{s=1} L(E,s),
\]

i.e. the order of vanishing of \(L(E,s)\) at \(s=1\).

So:

- if \(L(E,1)\neq 0\), then \(r_{\mathrm{an}}=0\);
- if \(L(E,1)=0\) but \(L'(E,1)\neq 0\), then \(r_{\mathrm{an}}=1\);
- if \(L(E,1)=L'(E,1)=0\) but \(L''(E,1)\neq 0\), then \(r_{\mathrm{an}}=2\);
- and so on.

The **weak Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture** says:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{rank} E(\mathbb{Q}) = \operatorname{ord}_{s=1} L(E,s).
}
\]

In words:

> The number of independent rational points of infinite order equals the order of vanishing of the \(L\)-function at \(s=1\).

So BSD predicts:

| Behavior of \(L(E,s)\) at \(s=1\) | Predicted arithmetic of \(E(\mathbb{Q})\) |
|---|---|
| \(L(E,1)\neq 0\) | rank \(0\), finitely many rational points |
| \(L(E,1)=0\), \(L'(E,1)\neq 0\) | rank \(1\), infinitely many rational points |
| \(L(E,1)=L'(E,1)=0\), \(L''(E,1)\neq 0\) | rank \(2\) |
| higher vanishing | higher rank |

For the curve

\[
E: y^2+y=x^3-x,
\]

one has

\[
L(E,1)=0,\qquad L'(E,1)\neq 0,
\]

so the analytic rank is \(1\), matching the algebraic rank \(1\).

---

## 4. The full BSD conjecture

The full conjecture is more precise. It predicts not only the rank, but also the exact leading coefficient of the Taylor expansion of \(L(E,s)\) at \(s=1\).

If

\[
r=\operatorname{rank}E(\mathbb{Q}),
\]

then near \(s=1\),

\[
L(E,s)
=
\frac{L^{(r)}(E,1)}{r!}(s-1)^r+\cdots
\]

BSD predicts the formula

\[
\boxed{
\frac{L^{(r)}(E,1)}{r!}
=
\frac{
\Omega_E \cdot R_E \cdot |\Sha(E/\mathbb{Q})| \cdot \prod_p c_p
}{
|E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}|^2
}.
}
\]

Here are the ingredients.

### 1. \(\Omega_E\): the real period

This is an integral over the real points of \(E\). Roughly, it measures the size of the real locus of the curve.

For a Weierstrass equation, it is obtained by integrating the Néron differential

\[
\omega = \frac{dx}{2y+a_1x+a_3}
\]

over appropriate real components.

---

### 2. \(R_E\): the regulator

The regulator is a determinant built from the canonical height pairing on a basis of the free part of \(E(\mathbb{Q})\).

If the rank is \(1\), generated by \(P\), then

\[
R_E=\hat{h}(P),
\]

where \(\hat{h}\) is the Néron–Tate canonical height.

For \(E: y^2+y=x^3-x\), the generator is \(P=(0,0)\), and

\[
R_E=\hat{h}(P)\approx 0.051111\ldots
\]

---

### 3. \(\Sha(E/\mathbb{Q})\): the Tate–Shafarevich group

This is one of the most mysterious objects in arithmetic geometry.

Roughly, \(\Sha\) measures the failure of the local-global principle for elliptic curves.

A curve may have points over every completion \(\mathbb{Q}_p\) and over \(\mathbb{R}\), but still fail to have rational points. The Tate–Shafarevich group records such obstructions for principal homogeneous spaces of \(E\).

BSD predicts that \(\Sha(E/\mathbb{Q})\) is finite.

The full formula predicts its exact order.

---

### 4. \(c_p\): Tamagawa numbers

For primes \(p\) where \(E\) has bad reduction, there is a local correction factor \(c_p\), called the **Tamagawa number**.

For

\[
E: y^2+y=x^3-x,
\]

the only bad prime is \(37\), and

\[
c_{37}=1.
\]

---

### 5. \(E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}\): torsion subgroup

This is the finite subgroup of rational points of finite order.

For

\[
E: y^2+y=x^3-x,
\]

the rational torsion subgroup is trivial:

\[
E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}=\{O\}.
\]

---

## 5. Example: the curve \(y^2+y=x^3-x\)

For

\[
E: y^2+y=x^3-x,
\]

one has:

\[
\operatorname{rank}E(\mathbb{Q})=1,
\]

\[
\operatorname{ord}_{s=1}L(E,s)=1,
\]

\[
E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}=0,
\]

\[
c_{37}=1,
\]

\[
R_E\approx 0.051111\ldots,
\]

and conjecturally

\[
|\Sha(E/\mathbb{Q})|=1.
\]

BSD predicts that

\[
L'(E,1)
=
\Omega_E \cdot R_E.
\]

Numerically this agrees to very high precision. In this case, the weak form of BSD is known because the analytic rank is \(1\).

---

## 6. Is BSD proven?

### In general: **No.**

The full Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture is still open.

It is one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems.

---

## 7. What is known?

### Analytic rank \(0\)

If

\[
L(E,1)\neq 0,
\]

then by results of Kolyvagin, building on work of Gross–Zagier and others, one knows that

\[
\operatorname{rank}E(\mathbb{Q})=0.
\]

So the weak BSD conjecture is known when the analytic rank is \(0\).

In particular, if the \(L\)-function does not vanish at \(s=1\), then the elliptic curve has only finitely many rational points.

---

### Analytic rank \(1\)

If

\[
L(E,1)=0
\quad\text{but}\quad
L'(E,1)\neq 0,
\]

then by the theorem of Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin, one knows that

\[
\operatorname{rank}E(\mathbb{Q})=1.
\]

So the weak BSD conjecture is also known when the analytic rank is \(1\).

In this case, one can construct a rational point of infinite order using **Heegner points**, and Kolyvagin’s Euler system machinery proves that the rank is exactly \(1\) and that \(\Sha\) is finite.

---

### Analytic rank \(\ge 2\)

For analytic rank \(2\) or higher, very little is known in general.

Even the weak statement

\[
\operatorname{rank}E(\mathbb{Q})
=
\operatorname{ord}_{s=1}L(E,s)
\]

is open in general for higher analytic rank.

The difficulty is that constructing enough rational points is extremely hard, and controlling the relevant Selmer groups is much more difficult.

---

### Full leading-term formula

The full formula involving \(\Omega_E\), \(R_E\), \(\Sha\), Tamagawa numbers, and torsion is known in many individual cases and for many families, but it is not known in complete generality.

Even for analytic rank \(0\) or \(1\), the weak rank equality is known, but the exact leading coefficient formula, especially the precise size of \(\Sha\), is not known for every elliptic curve over \(\mathbb{Q}\).

There are many important partial results, including:

- results for CM elliptic curves,
- \(p\)-adic BSD conjectures,
- \(p\)-part refinements of BSD,
- verification for many specific curves,
- average results toward BSD.

But the full conjecture remains open.

---

## 8. Why is BSD important?

BSD is important because it connects three worlds:

1. **Algebraic arithmetic**: rational points, ranks, Selmer groups, Tate–Shafarevich group.
2. **Analysis**: zeros and special values of \(L\)-functions.
3. **Geometry**: elliptic curves, heights, periods, reduction types.

It gives a conjectural formula for the rank, which is otherwise very hard to compute.

If BSD were fully proven, it would give a profound dictionary between:

\[
\text{zeros of } L(E,s)
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{rational points on } E.
\]

This is one of the central themes of modern number theory.

---

## 9. A useful summary

The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture says:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Rational points on an elliptic curve are controlled by the vanishing of its } L\text{-function at } s=1.
}
\]

More precisely:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{rank} E(\mathbb{Q})
=
\operatorname{ord}_{s=1} L(E,s).
}
\]

And the leading coefficient should satisfy:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{L^{(r)}(E,1)}{r!}
=
\frac{
\Omega_E R_E |\Sha(E/\mathbb{Q})| \prod_p c_p
}{
|E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}|^2
}.
}
\]

Its status is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Not proven in general.}
}
\]

But:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Known for analytic rank }0\text{ and }1\text{ in the weak sense over }\mathbb{Q}.
}
\]

For higher rank, it remains one of the deepest open problems in mathematics.
