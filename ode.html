Problem 1 — A Rotation-Masked Exact Equation: Logarithmic Spirals from a Hidden Integrating Factor

Equation and conditions

First-order equation in differential form:

$$ (2x - y)\,dx + (x + 2y)\,dy = 0, \qquad y(1) = 0. $$

Equivalently, in solved form,

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{2x - y}{x + 2y}, \qquad y(1) = 0. $$

Domain

  • The differential form is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$, but both coefficients vanish simultaneously only at the origin, so $(0,0)$ is the unique degenerate point and is excluded.
  • The solved form requires $x + 2y \neq 0$ (vertical tangents).
  • For the polar/integrating-factor computation, $x^2 + y^2 \neq 0$, i.e. the origin excluded.
  • The condition-selected solution is the logarithmic spiral $r = e^{-\theta/2}$ through $(1,0)$; as a function $y(x)$ it is valid on the maximal interval $$ x \in \Bigl(-(2/\sqrt5)\,e^{-(\pi-\alpha)/2},\ (2/\sqrt5)\,e^{\alpha/2}\Bigr), \qquad \alpha = \arctan(1/2), $$ i.e. approximately $x \in (-0.2344,\ 1.1278)$, where the spiral crosses the line $x+2y=0$ and the tangent becomes vertical.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: a homogeneous first-order equation $(M\,dx+N\,dy=0$ with $M,N$ linear, degree 1), reducible by $v = y/x$; geometrically the family of logarithmic spirals.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the equation is presented in rotation-masked form — the coefficient vector $(2x-y,\ x+2y)$ is the position vector $(x,y)$ rotated by the fixed angle $\phi = \arctan(1/2)$. The pedagogical point is the discovery and proof of the non-obvious geometric invariant: every solution meets each ray from the origin at the constant angle $\arctan 2$, which forces logarithmic spirals. The integrating factor $\mu = 1/(x^2+y^2)$ is likewise non-obvious (it depends on $x^2+y^2$, not on a ratio of $x,y$).
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: the specific rotation angle is not the point; the point is the structure (constant-angle curve family), which no affine change of variables can remove without destroying it — an affine change turns the spirals into a different family entirely, and rescaling only reparametrizes the radius. The equation is presented as a differential form whose exactness fails, so the key step is discovering the integrating factor, not recognizing a familiar solved form.

Anti-template audit: $v = y/x$ does reduce it (to $2\,dx/x + (1+2v)\,dv/(1+v^2)=0$, a routine separation) — however, the problem is retained because the pedagogical point is precisely the discovery and proof of the non-obvious invariant (the constant spiral angle / the rotation structure), which the prompt explicitly permits. Neither $u = ax+by$, $u = y^n$, $u = 1/y$, scaling alone, nor an exponential/logarithmic substitution reduces it any more directly than this; the polar-coordinate route $2\,dr + r\,d\theta = 0$ (Method 2) is the geometric reduction and is not on the routine list.

Solution method 1

Non-obvious integrating factor via the $\mu = \mu(x^2+y^2)$ criterion.

  1. Write $M = 2x - y$, $N = x + 2y$. The naive exactness test fails: $$ M_y = -1, \qquad N_x = 1, \qquad M_y \neq N_x. $$
  2. Test whether an integrating factor $\mu = \varphi(s)$ with $s = x^2 + y^2$ exists. From $(\mu M)_y = (\mu N)_x$: $$ 2y\,\varphi'(s)M + \varphi M_y = 2x\,\varphi'(s)N + \varphi N_x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M_y - N_x = 2\,\frac{\varphi'(s)}{\varphi(s)}\,(xN - yM). $$ Here $M_y - N_x = -2$ and $$ xN - yM = x(x+2y) - y(2x-y) = x^2 + y^2 = s. $$ Hence $$ -2 = 2\,\frac{\varphi'(s)}{\varphi(s)}\,s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\varphi'(s)}{\varphi(s)} = -\frac{1}{s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \varphi(s) = \frac{1}{s}. $$ So $\mu = \dfrac{1}{x^2+y^2}$.
  3. Multiply the differential form by $\mu$: $$ \frac{2x-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx + \frac{x+2y}{x^2+y^2}\,dy = 0, $$ which is now exact (recheck: both mixed derivatives equal $(y^2-x^2-4xy)/(x^2+y^2)^2$).
  4. Find a potential $F$ with $F_x = (2x-y)/(x^2+y^2)$, $F_y = (x+2y)/(x^2+y^2)$. Integrating $F_y$ with respect to $y$: $$ F = \int \frac{x+2y}{x^2+y^2}\,dy = x\int \frac{dy}{x^2+y^2} + \int \frac{2y\,dy}{x^2+y^2} = \arctan\frac{y}{x} + \ln(x^2+y^2) + \psi(x). $$ Then $$ F_x = -\frac{y}{x^2+y^2} + \frac{2x}{x^2+y^2} + \psi'(x) = \frac{2x-y}{x^2+y^2}, $$ so $\psi' = 0$. General solution: $$ \ln(x^2+y^2) + \arctan\frac{y}{x} = C . $$
  5. Apply $y(1) = 0$: $\ln 1 + \arctan 0 = 0$, so $C = 0$. Condition-selected solution: $$ \ln(x^2+y^2) + \arctan\frac{y}{x} = 0, $$ or, writing $x^2+y^2 = r^2$ and $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$: $$ x^2 + y^2 = e^{-\arctan(y/x)}. $$ (Undoing the last substitution: this is the level set of the potential through $(1,0)$.)

Solution method 2

Polar-coordinate reduction (geometric).

  1. Substitute $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, so $dx = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $dy = \sin\theta\,dr + r\cos\theta\,d\theta$.
  2. Substitute into the form: $$ (2x-y)\,dx + (x+2y)\,dy = (2r\cos\theta - r\sin\theta)(\cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta) + (r\cos\theta + 2r\sin\theta)(\sin\theta\,dr + r\cos\theta\,d\theta). $$
  3. Collect coefficients: $$ \text{coefficient of } dr:\quad (2c-s)c + (c+2s)s = 2c^2 - sc + sc + 2s^2 = 2, $$ $$ \text{coefficient of } d\theta:\quad -(2c-s)rs + (c+2s)rc = r(-2sc + s^2 + c^2 + 2sc) = r. $$ So the equation becomes $$ 2\,dr + r\,d\theta = 0. $$
  4. Separate and integrate: $$ \frac{dr}{r} = -\frac{d\theta}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = C\,e^{-\theta/2}. $$ This is the general solution in polar form: logarithmic spirals.
  5. Apply $y(1) = 0$, i.e. $(r,\theta) = (1,0)$: $C = 1$, giving $$ r = e^{-\theta/2}, $$ equivalently $2\ln r + \theta = 0$, i.e. $$ \ln(x^2+y^2) + \arctan\frac{y}{x} = 0 , $$ in agreement with Method 1.

Geometric interpretation: because $(2x-y,\ x+2y)$ is $(x,y)$ rotated by $\phi = \arctan(1/2)$, the tangent direction is the position vector rotated by $\phi + \pi/2$, so the angle between radius and tangent is $\pi/2 - \phi = \arctan 2$ everywhere — the defining property of a logarithmic spiral. (The homogeneity substitution $v = y/x$ yields the same family via $\int 2\,dx/x + \int (1+2v)\,dv/(1+v^2) = C$; it is noted here only as a cross-check, since it is the routine reduction.)

Verification

Differentiate $\ln(x^2+y^2) + \arctan(y/x) = 0$ implicitly:

$$ \frac{2x + 2y y'}{x^2+y^2} + \frac{y'x - y}{x^2}\,\frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2} = 0. $$

Multiply by $x^2 + y^2$:

$$ 2x + 2yy' + y'x - y = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y'(2y + x) = y - 2x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = \frac{y-2x}{x+2y} = -\frac{2x-y}{x+2y}, $$

which is exactly the original solved equation. The initial condition: at $(x,y) = (1,0)$, $\ln(1) + \arctan 0 = 0$ holds. Direct check that $\mu = 1/(x^2+y^2)$ renders the form exact was carried out in Method 1 step 3. The solution is valid on the stated domain because the spiral is a smooth curve avoiding the origin, and $x+2y\neq 0$ on the maximal interval given above.

Qualitative behavior

  • Equilibria/singular points: no equilibrium solutions exist; the only point where $M = N = 0$ is the origin, a degenerate singular point of the form. Every solution spirals into the origin as $\theta \to \infty$ (accumulating after winding infinitely often) — and does so in finite arc length: $s = \int_{\theta}^{\infty}\sqrt{r^2 + (dr/d\theta)^2}\,d\theta = (\sqrt5/2)\int_\theta^\infty e^{-\theta'/2}d\theta' = \sqrt5\,e^{-\theta/2}$.
  • Existence/uniqueness: near $(1,0)$ the solved form has $C^1$ right-hand side with $x+2y = 1 \neq 0$, so the IVP is locally unique; the solution extends until a vertical tangent.
  • Monotonicity/turning point: $y'(x) = 0$ where $2x - y = 0$, i.e. at $\theta = \arctan 2$, giving $(x,y) = (e^{-\arctan 2/2}\cos(\arctan 2),\ e^{-\arctan 2/2}\sin(\arctan 2)) \approx (0.2571,\ 0.5142)$. So $y(x)$ rises to a single maximum there and falls on either side.
  • Maximal interval: as a function $y(x)$, the branch through $(1,0)$ is valid on $x \in (-0.2344,\ 1.1278)$, with vertical tangents $(x+2y=0)$ at the two endpoints.
  • Asymptotics: $r \to 0$ as $\theta \to \infty$ (monotone winding toward the origin); $r \to \infty$ as $\theta \to -\infty$.

Final answer

$$ \ln(x^2+y^2) + \arctan\frac{y}{x} = 0, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad x^2 + y^2 = e^{-\arctan(y/x)}, \quad\text{or in polar form}\quad r = e^{-\theta/2}, \quad \text{valid on } x \in (-0.2344,\ 1.1278) $$

Problem 2 — A Coupled $xy$-Structure with a Singular Envelope: the Substitution $u = xy$

Equation and conditions

First-order equation:

$$ y' = \frac{y}{x}\,\frac{1 - 2xy}{1 + xy}, \qquad x \gt 0, \qquad y(1) = 1. $$

Domain

  • $x \gt 0$ (the equation contains $y/x$); $1 + xy \neq 0$ is required for the solved form.
  • The condition-selected solution satisfies $xy \in (0,2)$ for all $x \in (0,\infty)$, so $1+xy \neq 0$ automatically; the maximal interval is $(0,\infty)$.
  • The line $xy = 2$, i.e. $y = 2/x$, is a singular solution (envelope) approached asymptotically; the line $y = 0$ is the degenerate member of the general family.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: homogeneous-looking first-order equations and Bernoulli equations; the true structure is a first-order equation invariant under the product structure $xy$.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the equation is not separable, not homogeneous, not Bernoulli, and not linear in $x$ or $y$ (checked below); the multiplicative coupling $(1-2xy)/(1+xy)$ of $x,y,y'$ is what enables the non-obvious substitution $u = xy$, and the resulting separable equation $u' = (u/x)(2-u)/(1+u)$ has the non-obvious first integral $u/(2-u)^3 x^{-2} = K$, plus a genuine singular solution $y = 2/x$.
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: the constants $1,2$ in $(1-2xy)/(1+xy)$ are not the novelty; removing the product coupling (e.g. setting $xy = c$ constant) collapses the equation to $y' = \text{const}\cdot y/x$, a different (routine) equation. No affine or scaling change removes the $xy$-coupling — it is the structure.

Anti-template audit: $v = y/x$ fails (it leaves an $x$-dependent factor: $xv' = -3x^2v^2/(1+x^2v)$); $u = 1/y$ fails; $u = ax+by$ fails (no $ax+by$ combination occurs); logarithmic/exponential substitutions fail; separation fails because $(1-2xy)/(1+xy)$ couples $x$ and $y$ nontrivially. The substitution $u = xy$ is not on the prompt's routine list ($u = y^n$, $u=1/y$, $u=y/x$, $u=ax+by$, log/exp).

Solution method 1

Substitution $u = xy$.

  1. Set $u = xy$, so $y = u/x$ and $u' = y + xy'$.
  2. Then $y' = (u/x)(1-2u)/(1+u)$, and $$ u' = \frac{u}{x} + x\cdot \frac{u}{x}\,\frac{1-2u}{1+u} = \frac{u}{x}\left(1 + \frac{1-2u}{1+u}\right) = \frac{u}{x}\cdot \frac{2-u}{1+u}. $$
  3. This separates: $$ \frac{(1+u)\,du}{u(2-u)} = \frac{dx}{x}. $$
  4. Partial fractions: $(1+u)/(u(2-u)) = (1/2)/u + (3/2)/(2-u)$, so $$ \frac12 \ln u - \frac32 \ln(2-u) = \ln x + C_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{u}{(2-u)^3} = Kx^2, \qquad K \gt 0. $$
  5. Undo the substitution: the general solution is $$ \frac{xy}{(2-xy)^3} = K x^2. $$
  6. Apply $y(1) = 1$, i.e. $u(1) = 1$: $1/(2-1)^3 = K$, so $K = 1$. Condition-selected solution:
    $$ \frac{xy}{(2-xy)^3} = x^2, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad xy = x^2\,(2-xy)^3. $$
    (The singular solution $y = 2/x$, corresponding formally to $K \to \infty$, is not in the general family; $y \equiv 0$ corresponds to $K = 0$.)

Solution method 2

Independent Taylor (power-series) derivation about $x = 1$, cross-checked against the implicit solution.

  1. Write $y = \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n (x-1)^n$ with $a_0 = 1$. Evaluate the ODE at $x=1$: $$ y'(1) = \frac{1}{1}\cdot\frac{1-2}{1+1} = -\frac12, $$ so $a_1 = -1/2$.
  2. Differentiate the ODE. With $f = y/x$ and $g = (1-2xy)/(1+xy)$, $y' = fg$. At $x=1$: $f = 1$, $f' = (y'x - y)/x^2 = -3/2$, $g = -1/2$, and $$ g' = \frac{(-2y-2xy')(1+xy) - (1-2xy)(y+xy')}{(1+xy)^2}\Big|_{x=1} = \frac{(-1)(2) - (-1)(1/2)}{4} = -\frac38 . $$ Then $y''(1) = f'g + fg' = (-3/2)(-1/2) + (1)(-3/8) = 3/8$, so $a_2 = 3/16$.
  3. One more derivative. With $f'' = (y'' - 2y' + 2y)/x^3$, $f''(1) = 27/8$; and with $N = (-2y-2xy')(1+xy) - (1-2xy)(y+xy')$, $D = 1+xy$, one computes $N(1) = -3/2$, $N'(1) = 15/8$, $D(1) = 2$, $D'(1) = 1/2$, so $$ g''(1) = \frac{N'D - 2ND'}{D^3}\Big|_{x=1} = \frac{15}{32} + \frac{3}{16} = \frac{21}{32}. $$ Thus $y'''(1) = f''g + 2f'g' + fg'' = (27/8)(-1/2) + 2(-3/2)(-3/8) + 21/32 = 3/32$, so $a_3 = 1/64$. Hence $$ y(x) = 1 - \frac{x-1}{2} + \frac{3}{16}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{64}(x-1)^3 + O\bigl((x-1)^4\bigr). $$
  4. Independent check against the implicit solution: with $t = x-1$ and $u = xy$, $$ u = (1+t)\bigl(1 - t/2 + (3/16)t^2 + (1/64)t^3 + \cdots\bigr) = 1 + \frac{t}{2} - \frac{5}{16}t^2 + \frac{13}{64}t^3 + \cdots $$ Substituting into $u = x^2(2-u)^3$ and matching coefficients of $t, t^2, t^3$ reproduces exactly $a_1 = -1/2$, $a_2 = 3/16$, $a_3 = 1/64$. The two derivations agree term by term.

Verification

Differentiate the implicit solution $xy/(2-xy)^3 = x^2$:

$$ \frac{d}{du}\!\left(\frac{u}{(2-u)^3}\right) = \frac{2(1+u)}{(2-u)^4}, $$

so with $u = xy$,

$$ \frac{2(1+u)}{(2-u)^4}\,u' = 2x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u' = \frac{x(2-u)^4}{1+u} = \frac{x(2-u)(2-u)^3}{1+u} = \frac{x(2-u)\cdot u/(x^2)}{1+u} = \frac{u}{x}\,\frac{2-u}{1+u}, $$

where $(2-u)^3 = u/x^2$ was used (the relation being differentiated). But $u' = (u/x)(2-u)/(1+u)$ is exactly the reduced ODE of Method 1, equivalent to the original $y' = (y/x)(1-2xy)/(1+xy)$. Initial condition: at $(1,1)$, $xy/(2-xy)^3 = 1 = x^2$. Singular solution check: $y = 2/x$ gives $y' = -2/x^2$ and RHS $= (2/x^2)(1-4)/(1+2) = -2/x^2$. Valid on $x \gt 0$, where $0 \lt xy \lt 2$ throughout.

Qualitative behavior

  • Equilibria/singular solutions: $y \equiv 0$ is the $K=0$ member of the family; $y = 2/x$ is a singular solution not obtainable from the family — it is the envelope where $u = 2$, i.e. where the separated ODE degenerates.
  • Existence/uniqueness: the RHS is $C^1$ on $x \gt 0$, $1+xy \neq 0$, so solutions are unique there; the solution through $(1,1)$ stays in the strip $0 \lt xy \lt 2$, so no singular surface is hit.
  • Monotonicity/turning point: $y'(x)$ has the sign of $1-2u$; $u = 1/2$ occurs at $x_0 = 2/(3\sqrt3) \approx 0.3849$, where $y$ attains its unique maximum $y_{\max} = 3\sqrt3/4 \approx 1.2990$.
  • Asymptotics: as $x \to 0^+$, $u/(2-u)^3 \sim u/8 = x^2$ gives $y \sim 8x \to 0$; as $x \to \infty$, $2-u \to 0$ and $y \to 2/x$ from below (the solution is asymptotic to the singular solution).
  • Maximal interval: $(0,\infty)$.

Final answer

$$ \frac{xy}{(2-xy)^3} = x^2, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad xy = x^2(2-xy)^3, \qquad x \in (0,\infty) $$

Problem 3 — An Autonomous Equation with a One-Sided Asymptotic Equilibrium

Equation and conditions

First-order autonomous equation:

$$ y' = \frac{(y-1)(y^2+1)}{y^2 + y + 1}, \qquad y(0) = 0. $$

Domain

  • All of $\mathbb{R}^2$: the denominator $y^2 + y + 1 = (y + 1/2)^2 + 3/4 \gt 0$ for all real $y$, so the right-hand side is $C^\infty$ and bounded-away-from-zero in the denominator everywhere.
  • The solution through $(0,0)$ lies in $y \lt 1$ for all $x$ and is defined for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • Branch choices: for the solution through $(0,0)$ we use the branch $\ln(1-y)$ with $y \lt 1$.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: separable autonomous equations $y' = f(y)$ with a rational $f$.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: although the separation is immediate, the analysis is not routine: the integral requires a three-term partial-fraction decomposition mixing a logarithm of a quadratic, an arctangent, and a logarithm of a linear factor, and the equilibrium $y = 1$ is one-sided: solutions approach it only from below as $x \to -\infty$, while from above they escape to $+\infty$. The phase-line analysis is the primary deliverable, and the explicit $x(y)$ is implicit in $y$ (no elementary inverse).
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: replacing $(y-1)(y^2+1)/(y^2+y+1)$ by a translate of a standard $f$ changes the partial-fraction structure qualitatively (the denominator is chosen so it does not factor over $\mathbb{R}$, which is what forces the arctangent term); the mixed log/arctan answer is the structural feature, not the coefficients.

Anti-template audit: none of $u = y^n$, $u = 1/y$, $u = y/x$, $u = ax+by$ applies (there is no $x$ in the equation and no routine substitution simplifies a single-variable rational function); the equation is separable only in the trivial autonomous sense, and this is deliberately one of the (at most two) separable equations in the set.

Solution method 1

Separation with partial fractions.

  1. Separate: $$ \frac{y^2 + y + 1}{(y-1)(y^2+1)}\,dy = dx . $$
  2. Partial fractions: $$ \frac{y^2+y+1}{(y-1)(y^2+1)} = \frac{-\frac12 y + \frac12}{y^2+1} + \frac{\frac32}{y-1}, $$ verified by clearing denominators: $(-\tfrac12 y + \tfrac12)(y-1) + \tfrac32(y^2+1) = y^2 + y + 1$.
  3. Integrate: $$ x = -\frac14 \ln(y^2+1) + \frac12 \arctan y + \frac32 \ln|y-1| + C . $$
  4. Apply $y(0) = 0$: $0 = 0 + 0 + 0 + C$, so $C = 0$. Since $y(0) = 0 \lt 1$, the solution stays in $y \lt 1$, and the condition-selected solution is $$ x = -\frac14\ln(1+y^2) + \frac12\arctan y + \frac32\ln(1-y), \qquad y \in (-\infty, 1). $$
  5. This is the final form: explicit in $x$ as a function of $y$, implicit in $y$. The general solution is the same display with arbitrary $C$ (with $|y-1|$ and $y$ on the appropriate side of 1).

Solution method 2

Independent phase-line analysis (no integration), which re-derives all qualitative conclusions from $f$ alone.

  1. Equilibria: $f(y) = 0 \iff y = 1$ (since $y^2+1$ and $y^2+y+1$ have no real zeros).
  2. Stability by linearization: $$ f'(y) = \frac{\bigl[(y^2+1) + 2y(y-1)\bigr](y^2+y+1) - (y-1)(y^2+1)(2y+1)}{(y^2+y+1)^2}, \qquad f'(1) = \frac{2\cdot 3}{9} = \frac23 \gt 0, $$ so $y = 1$ is an unstable equilibrium (repelling).
  3. Sign analysis: $\operatorname{sgn} f(y) = \operatorname{sgn}(y-1)$. Hence: for $y \lt 1$, solutions decrease ($y' \lt 0$); for $y \gt 1$, solutions increase ($y' \gt 0$).
  4. Asymptotics without integrating. Near $y = 1$: $f(y) \sim \frac23 (y-1)$, so solutions below 1 satisfy $y - 1 \sim C e^{2x/3} \to 0^-$ as $x \to -\infty$ — approached only from below, at exponential rate. As $y \to \pm\infty$: $f(y) \sim y$, so $\int dy/f$ diverges like $\ln|y|$, i.e. no finite-$x$ blow-up: the maximal interval is $\mathbb{R}$. For the branch with $y(0) = 0 \lt 1$: $y \to 1^-$ as $x \to -\infty$, $y \to -\infty$ (like $-e^{x}$) as $x \to +\infty$. For $y(0) \gt 1$: $y \to 1^+$ as $x \to -\infty$ and $y \to +\infty$ (like $e^{x}$) as $x \to +\infty$.
  5. Existence/uniqueness: $f \in C^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $f'$ is bounded on compact sets, so the IVP has a unique solution for every real initial datum; all solutions exist on all of $\mathbb{R}$.
  6. These conclusions match the explicit solution of Method 1 exactly (see Verification).

Verification

Differentiate the implicit solution with respect to $y$:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = -\frac{y/2}{1+y^2} + \frac{1/2}{1+y^2} - \frac{3/2}{1-y} = \frac{1-y}{2(1+y^2)} - \frac{3}{2(1-y)} = \frac{(1-y)^2 - 3(1+y^2)}{2(1+y^2)(1-y)} $$ $$ = \frac{1 - 2y + y^2 - 3 - 3y^2}{2(1+y^2)(1-y)} = \frac{-2(y^2+y+1)}{2(1+y^2)(1-y)} = \frac{y^2+y+1}{(y-1)(y^2+1)} . $$

Hence

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{(y-1)(y^2+1)}{y^2+y+1}, $$

the original equation. Initial condition: at $y = 0$, $x = 0$ (all terms vanish). The solution is valid on all of $\mathbb{R}$ with $y \in (-\infty, 1)$, as the phase-line analysis predicts, and the displayed branch satisfies $\lim_{x\to-\infty} y = 1^-$, $\lim_{x\to+\infty} y = -\infty$.

Qualitative behavior

  • Equilibria: $y \equiv 1$ only; unstable ($f'(1) = 2/3 \gt 0$); no other constant solutions; no singular solutions (the ODE is nowhere singular).
  • Existence/uniqueness: global (see above); no blow-up in finite $x$.
  • Monotonicity: solutions are monotone on each side of the equilibrium; no turning points ($f$ never changes sign except across $y=1$).
  • Asymptotics: $y(0)=0$ branch: $y \nearrow 1$ as $x \to -\infty$ (rate $e^{2x/3}$), $y \searrow -\infty$ as $x \to +\infty$ (rate $-e^x$).
  • Maximal interval: $\mathbb{R}$ for every solution.

Final answer

$$ x \;=\; -\frac14\ln(1+y^2) \;+\; \frac12\arctan y \;+\; \frac32\ln(1-y), \qquad y \in (-\infty, 1), \qquad x \in \mathbb{R} $$

Problem 4 — A Similarity-Invariant Equation under $(x,y) \mapsto (\lambda x, \lambda^2 y)$

Equation and conditions

First-order equation:

$$ y' = \frac{y}{x}\,\frac{4y + 3x^2}{y + x^2}, \qquad y(1) = 1, $$

equivalently the differential form

$$ y(4y + 3x^2)\,dx - x(y + x^2)\,dy = 0. $$

Domain

  • The solved form requires $x \neq 0$ and $y + x^2 \neq 0$.
  • The condition-selected solution has $y \gt 0$ and $y + x^2 \gt 0$ for all $x \gt 0$; its maximal interval is $x \in (0, \infty)$.
  • The integrating-factor route (Method 2) additionally requires $y \neq 0$, satisfied on the selected solution.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: homogeneous equations (invariant under $x,y \mapsto \lambda x, \lambda y$) and Bernoulli equations.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the equation is not homogeneous in the standard sense; it is invariant under the two-weight scaling $(x,y) \mapsto (\lambda x, \lambda^2 y)$, a similarity group whose invariant is $u = y/x^2$ — a genuinely non-obvious reduction because the standard homogeneous substitution $v = y/x$ provably fails. Moreover the same equation admits an integrating factor $\mu = x^3 y^{-3}$ of mixed power type, discoverable by the $x^m y^n$ ansatz, which yields a second, independent solution route.
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: the structural novelty is the mismatch between the $x$-weight and $y$-weight (the $3x^2$ versus $4y$ coefficients), which is exactly what breaks ordinary homogeneity; changing the constants $4,3$ to other values only rescales the $u$-equation $xu' = u(2u+1)/(u+1)$ and does not alter the structure.

Anti-template audit: $v = y/x$ fails ($xv' = v(4v+3x^2)/(v+x^2) - v$ retains $x$); $u = 1/y$ fails; $u = y^n$ fails (the $(4y+3x^2)/(y+x^2)$ factor is not a function of $y$ alone); $u = ax+by$ fails; the equation is not separable and not Bernoulli (the $1/(y+x^2)$ factor puts $y$ in the denominator, so $y'$ cannot be written $p(x)y + q(x)y^n$); it is not linear in either variable. The successful substitution $u = y/x^2$ is not on the routine list.

Solution method 1

Similarity (scaling-symmetry) reduction.

  1. Symmetry check: under $(x,y) \mapsto (\lambda x, \lambda^2 y)$ the differential form scales by $\lambda^5$: $y(4y+3x^2) \mapsto \lambda^4 y(4y+3x^2)$ with $dx \mapsto \lambda\,dx$, and $x(y+x^2) \mapsto \lambda^3 x(y+x^2)$ with $dy \mapsto \lambda^2\,dy$. Invariance means the substitution $y = x^2 u$ must eliminate $x$.
  2. Set $y = x^2 u$, so $y' = 2xu + x^2 u'$. Substitute into the solved form: $$ 2xu + x^2 u' = \frac{x^2u}{x}\,\frac{4x^2u + 3x^2}{x^2u + x^2} = xu\,\frac{4u+3}{u+1}. $$
  3. Rearrange: $$ x u' = u\frac{4u+3}{u+1} - 2u = \frac{u(4u+3) - 2u(u+1)}{u+1} = \frac{u(2u+1)}{u+1}. $$
  4. Separate: $$ \frac{(u+1)\,du}{u(2u+1)} = \frac{dx}{x}. $$
  5. Partial fractions: $(u+1)/(u(2u+1)) = 1/u - 1/(2u+1)$, so $$ \ln u - \frac12 \ln(2u+1) = \ln x + C_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{u}{\sqrt{2u+1}} = Kx, \qquad K \gt 0. $$
  6. Solve the quadratic $u^2 = K^2x^2(2u+1)$: $$ u = K^2x^2 + Kx\sqrt{K^2x^2 + 1} $$ (the plus branch, consistent with $u \gt 0$ for $Kx \gt 0$). Undo the substitution: $$ y = x^2u = K^2x^4 + Kx^3\sqrt{K^2x^2 + 1} . $$
  7. Apply $y(1) = 1$: $1 = K^2 + K\sqrt{K^2+1}$. Squaring: $K^2(K^2+1) = (1-K^2)^2 \Rightarrow 3K^2 = 1$, so $K = 1/\sqrt3$ (positive branch). Condition-selected solution: $$ y = \frac{1}{3}x^4 + \frac{1}{\sqrt3}x^3\sqrt{\frac{x^2}{3} + 1} = \frac{x^3}{3}\Bigl(x + \sqrt{x^2 + 3}\Bigr). $$

Solution method 2

Hidden integrating factor of power type $x^m y^n$.

  1. From the form $M\,dx + N\,dy = 0$ with $M = y(4y+3x^2)$, $N = -x(y+x^2)$: $M_y = 8y + 3x^2$, $N_x = -y - 3x^2$, so $M_y - N_x = 9y + 6x^2 \neq 0$ — not exact.
  2. Try $\mu = x^m y^n$. The condition $(\mu M)_y = (\mu N)_x$, i.e. $(n/y)M + M_y = (m/x)N + N_x$, becomes $$ n(4y + 3x^2) + 8y + 3x^2 = m(-y - x^2) - y - 3x^2 . $$
  3. Compare coefficients of $y$ and $x^2$: $$ 4n + 8 = -m - 1, \qquad 3n + 3 = -m - 3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m + 4n = -9, \quad m + 3n = -6, $$ so $n = -3$, $m = 3$: the integrating factor is $$ \mu = \frac{x^3}{y^3}. $$
  4. Multiply the form: $$ \left(\frac{4x^3}{y} + \frac{3x^5}{y^2}\right)dx - \left(\frac{x^4}{y^2} + \frac{x^6}{y^3}\right)dy = 0, $$ which is exact (both mixed derivatives equal $-4x^3/y^2 - 6x^5/y^3$).
  5. Potential: $F(x,y) = \dfrac{x^4}{y} + \dfrac{x^6}{2y^2}$ (check: $F_x = 4x^3/y + 3x^5/y^2$; $F_y = -x^4/y^2 - x^6/y^3$). General solution: $$ \frac{x^4}{y} + \frac{x^6}{2y^2} = C. $$
  6. Solve for $y$: $2Cy^2 - 2x^4y - x^6 = 0$, giving $$ y = \frac{x^4 + x^3\sqrt{x^2 + 2C}}{2C} $$ — with $2C = 1/K^2$ this is the family of Method 1: $y = K^2x^4 + Kx^3\sqrt{K^2x^2+1}$.
  7. Apply $y(1)=1$: $x^4/y + x^6/(2y^2)\big|_{(1,1)} = 1 + 1/2 = 3/2 = C$. Then $2C = 3$, $K = 1/\sqrt3$ as before, and $y = (x^3/3)(x + \sqrt{x^2+3})$.

Verification

From the form $y(4y+3x^2)dx - x(y+x^2)dy = 0$:

$$ x(y+x^2)\,\frac{dy}{dx} = y(4y+3x^2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = \frac{y}{x}\,\frac{4y+3x^2}{y+x^2}, $$

which is the original equation; and multiplying by $x^3/y^3$ turns it into $d(x^4/y + x^6/(2y^2)) = 0$ (checked in Method 2, step 4). Independent direct check of the final answer: with $S = \sqrt{x^2+3}$ and $y = (x^3/3)(x+S)$, the identity $x(2x + S + x^2/S) = u(2u+1)/(u+1)\cdot 3/(x)$ with $u = (x/3)(x+S)$ reduces — using $S^2 = x^2+3$ — to $S^2 = x^2 + 3$, so $xu' = u(2u+1)/(u+1)$ holds exactly, and the reverse substitution gives the original ODE. Initial condition: $y(1) = (1/3)(1+2) = 1$. Valid on $x \in (0,\infty)$ where $y \gt 0$, $y + x^2 \gt 0$.

Qualitative behavior

  • Equilibria: none (the equation is non-autonomous); $y \equiv 0$ is not a solution of the solved form (division by $y+x^2 = x^2$ would give $0 = 0$ — in fact $y\equiv 0$ does satisfy $y(4y+3x^2)dx - x(y+x^2)dy = 0$ trivially, but the selected solution is the positive branch). Singular surfaces: $y + x^2 = 0$ and $x = 0$ bound the domain.
  • Existence/uniqueness: the RHS is $C^1$ near $(1,1)$ with $y+x^2 = 2 \neq 0$, so the IVP is locally unique.
  • Monotonicity: $y' = (y/x)(4y+3x^2)/(y+x^2) \gt 0$ on the solution, so $y$ is strictly increasing; no turning points.
  • Asymptotics: as $x \to 0^+$, $y = (x^3/3)(x + \sqrt{x^2+3}) \sim (\sqrt3/3)x^3 \to 0$; as $x \to \infty$, $y \sim \frac23 x^4$ (superlinear growth). The $K \lt 0$ members of the family give negative solutions with the same asymptotics.
  • Maximal interval: $(0,\infty)$.

Final answer

$$ y(x) = \frac{x^3}{3}\Bigl(x + \sqrt{x^2+3}\Bigr), \qquad x \in (0,\infty). \quad\text{General solution:}\quad y = K^2x^4 + Kx^3\sqrt{K^2x^2+1} $$

Problem 5 — A Nonlinear Second-Order Equation Linearized by $\tan y$

Equation and conditions

Second-order equation:

$$ y'' = -2\,(y')^2 \tan y, \qquad y(0) = 0, \quad y'(0) = 1. $$

Domain

  • The right-hand side is defined for $y \neq (2m+1)\pi/2$ ($m \in \mathbb{Z}$) and is real-analytic there; the IVP data lie in the strip $|y| \lt \pi/2$.
  • The condition-selected solution $y = \arctan x$ has $|y| \lt \pi/2$ and is defined for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • The constant solutions $y \equiv c$, $c \neq (2m+1)\pi/2$, are the equilibrium solutions.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: second-order autonomous (missing-$x$) equations $y'' = g(y)(y')^2$ reducible by $p = p(y)$, and the Riccati-type structure $u'' = (u')^2/u$.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the trigonometric factor $\tan y$ is chosen so that the nonlinearity is exactly the curvature term of the transformation $z = \tan y$: $z'' = \sec^2 y\,(y'' + 2(y')^2\tan y)$. The equation is the obstruction to $z$ being linear; the insight is that the nonlinear second-order equation is linearized by a dependent-variable change into $z'' = 0$. The reduction-of-order route is also non-routine: $p\,dp/dy = -2p^2\tan y$ integrates to $p = K\cos^2 y$ rather than to a familiar first integral.
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: replacing the coefficient $-2$ by another constant merely rescales $\tan y \to \alpha \tan y$ and changes the linearized equation to $z'' = (2-\alpha)(y')^2\sec^2 y \tan y$-type structure only for special values; the coefficient $-2$ is forced by the exact linearization $z''=0$, and the equation itself is not a standard named exercise (it is not $y''=f(y)$, not a Riccati equation, and not linear in any of $y,y',y''$).

Anti-template audit: the routine substitutions ($u = y^n$, $u=1/y$, $u=y/x$, $u=ax+by$, log/exp) do not reduce it; the standard autonomous trick $p(y) = y'$ does reduce it (this is Method 1, and it is the intended advanced-undergraduate technique for missing-$x$ equations), but the resulting equation $dp/dy = -2p\tan y$ is not a classroom template, and the independent second method (change of dependent variable) is a genuinely different derivation, not a restatement.

Solution method 1

Reduction of order via $p(y) = y'$ (missing $x$).

  1. With $p = y'$ and $y'' = p\,dp/dy$: $$ p\,\frac{dp}{dy} = -2p^2\tan y . $$
  2. For $p \neq 0$ (the solution with $y'(0)=1$ has $p \neq 0$): $$ \frac{dp}{p} = -2\tan y\,dy \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln|p| = 2\ln|\cos y| + C_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = K\cos^2 y . $$
  3. Undo the substitution: $y' = K\cos^2 y$, i.e. $$ \frac{dy}{\cos^2 y} = K\,dx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan y = Kx + C . $$
  4. Undo the second substitution: the general solution is $$ y = \arctan(Kx + C) + m\pi, \qquad m \in \mathbb{Z}, $$ together with the constant solutions $y \equiv c$ (the case $p \equiv 0$).
  5. Apply the conditions: $y(0) = 0$ selects $m = 0$, $C = 0$; $y'(0) = 1$ gives $K\cos^2 0 = K = 1$. Condition-selected solution:
    $$ y = \arctan x, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}. $$

Solution method 2

Change of dependent variable $z = \tan y$.

  1. Differentiate: $$ z' = y'\sec^2 y, \qquad z'' = y''\sec^2 y + y'\cdot 2\sec y\,(\sec y\tan y)\cdot y' = \sec^2 y \bigl(y'' + 2(y')^2\tan y\bigr). $$
  2. The original equation states exactly that the bracket vanishes, so $$ z'' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = ax + b. $$
  3. Undo the substitution: $\tan y = ax + b$, so $y = \arctan(ax+b) + m\pi$ — the same general solution as Method 1, obtained by a completely different route (a dependent-variable change rather than a reduction of order).
  4. Conditions: $\tan y(0) = b = 0$; $z'(0) = y'(0)\sec^2 y(0) = 1\cdot 1 = a = 1$. Hence $z = x$, i.e. $y = \arctan x$.

Verification

For $y = \arctan x$: $y' = 1/(1+x^2)$, $y'' = -2x/(1+x^2)^2$, and

$$ -2(y')^2\tan y = -2\,\frac{1}{(1+x^2)^2}\cdot x = -\frac{2x}{(1+x^2)^2} = y''. $$

Initial conditions: $y(0) = \arctan 0 = 0$, $y'(0) = 1/(1+0) = 1$. General-solution check: for $y = \arctan(C_1x + C_2)$, $y' = C_1/(1+t^2)$, $y'' = -2C_1^2t/(1+t^2)^2 = -2(y')^2\tan y$, with $t = C_1x+C_2$. Validity: $\arctan x \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ for all $x$, where $\tan$ is analytic, so the solution is valid on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

Qualitative behavior

  • Equilibria: every constant $y \equiv c$ with $\tan c$ defined is a solution — a continuum of equilibria; the lines $y = (2m+1)\pi/2$ are outside the domain of the equation (not solutions).
  • Existence/uniqueness: the RHS is $C^\infty$ near $(y, y') = (0, 1)$, so the IVP is locally unique; the solution extends to all of $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Monotonicity/concavity: $y'(x) = 1/(1+x^2) \gt 0$ (increasing); $y'' = -2x/(1+x^2)^2$, so $y$ is convex for $x \lt 0$, concave for $x \gt 0$, with an inflection point at $(0,0)$ — the only inflection point.
  • Asymptotics: $y \to \pm \pi/2$ as $x \to \pm\infty$ (the asymptote lines are approached but never attained); no blow-up, no oscillation.
  • Maximal interval: $\mathbb{R}$.

Final answer

$$ y(x) = \arctan x, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}. \qquad \text{General solution:}\quad y = m\pi + \arctan(C_1 x + C_2),\quad m\in\mathbb{Z},\ \text{plus}\ y \equiv c $$

Problem 6 — Gaussian Solutions from a Conserved Quantity

Equation and conditions

Second-order equation:

$$ y'' = \frac{(y')^2}{y} - \frac{y}{2}, \qquad y(0) = 1, \quad y'(0) = 0. $$

Domain

  • $y \neq 0$ is required (division by $y$); we work on the half-planes $y \gt 0$ and $y \lt 0$ separately, with the natural extension $\ln|y|$ in the first integral.
  • The condition-selected solution is $y = e^{-x^2/4} \gt 0$, defined for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: autonomous second-order equations with a conserved energy, and equations of Riccati-Bernoulli type in $p$.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the conserved quantity $$ I(y, y') = \frac{(y')^2}{y^2} + \ln y $$ is a genuinely non-obvious first integral (it is not the standard "kinetic plus potential" energy, and the potential part is logarithmic). Every nontrivial solution is a Gaussian $A\,e^{-(x-B)^2/4}$: a family of solutions with a translation-and-scale symmetry of unusual type. The phase portrait consists of infinite-period loops through the degenerate point $(0,0)$, where the vector field is undefined.
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: the structure is the homogeneity of the two terms $(y')^2/y$ and $y/2$ under $y \mapsto cy$ — which is what makes $(y'/y)'$ appear; changing the coefficient $1/2$ to another constant rescales the width of the Gaussian but does not change the mechanism, whereas removing either term collapses the equation to $y'' = (y')^2/y$ (pure exponentials, a different standard equation) or to the harmonic oscillator. The novelty is the combination, which forces the logarithmic first integral.

Anti-template audit: none of the routine first-order substitutions applies (the equation is second order); the standard autonomous reduction $p(y)$ turns it into a Bernoulli equation $dp/dy - p/y = -(y/2)p^{-1}$ — solvable, but the reduction and its Bernoulli step are exactly the "embedded in a genuinely new structure" case: the new structure is the conserved quantity and the Gaussian family, not the Bernoulli template itself. The equation is not the harmonic oscillator, not $y'' = f(y)$ with an elementary potential, and not any standard named equation.

Solution method 1

Reduction of order and discovery of the conserved quantity.

  1. Set $p(y) = y'$, so $y'' = p\,dp/dy$: $$ p\,\frac{dp}{dy} = \frac{p^2}{y} - \frac{y}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dp}{dy} - \frac{1}{y}\,p = -\frac{y}{2}\,p^{-1}, $$ a Bernoulli equation in $p$ (exponent $-1$).
  2. Multiply by $p$ and set $q = p^2$: $$ \frac12\,\frac{dq}{dy} - \frac{q}{y} = -\frac{y}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dq}{dy} - \frac{2}{y}\,q = -y , $$ a linear equation for $q$.
  3. Integrating factor $\mu = e^{-\int 2\,dy/y} = y^{-2}$: $$ \frac{d}{dy}\!\left(\frac{q}{y^2}\right) = -\frac{1}{y} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{p^2}{y^2} = -\ln y + C . $$ This is the conserved quantity: $$ \frac{(y')^2}{y^2} + \ln y = C . $$
  4. Undo the substitution: $y' = \pm y\sqrt{C - \ln y}$, so $$ \frac{dy}{y\sqrt{C - \ln y}} = \pm\,dx . $$
  5. Integrate with $u = C - \ln y$, $du = -dy/y$: $$ -2\sqrt{C - \ln y} = \pm x + B \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt{C - \ln y} = C_1 \mp \frac{x}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln y = C - \Bigl(C_1 \mp \frac{x}{2}\Bigr)^2 . $$ The general solution is $$ y = A\,e^{-(x - B)^2/4}, \qquad A \neq 0,\ B \in \mathbb{R}, $$ together with $y \equiv 0$ (the degenerate solution).
  6. Apply the conditions: $y'(0)/y(0) = 0$ gives $B = 0$; $y(0) = A = 1$. Condition-selected solution:
    $$ y = e^{-x^2/4}. $$

Solution method 2

Direct integration of the logarithmic-derivative structure.

  1. Divide the equation by $y$ (nonzero): $$ \frac{y''}{y} - \frac{(y')^2}{y^2} = -\frac12 . $$
  2. The left side is exactly the derivative of $y'/y$: $$ \left(\frac{y'}{y}\right)' = \frac{y''}{y} - \frac{(y')^2}{y^2} . $$
  3. Hence $$ \left(\frac{y'}{y}\right)' = -\frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{y'}{y} = C_1 - \frac{x}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln|y| = C_1 x - \frac{x^2}{4} + C_2 . $$
  4. Exponentiate: $$ y = A\,e^{C_1x - x^2/4} = A\,e^{-(x - 2C_1)^2/4}\,e^{C_1^2}, $$ the same two-parameter Gaussian family $y = A\,e^{-(x-B)^2/4}$ (absorb $e^{C_1^2}$ into $A$).
  5. Conditions: $y'(0)/y(0) = C_1 = 0$, $y(0) = e^{C_2} = 1$: $y = e^{-x^2/4}$.

This is genuinely independent of Method 1: it uses no reduction of order and no Bernoulli step, only the recognition that $y''/y - (y')^2/y^2$ is a logarithmic derivative.

Verification

For $y = e^{-x^2/4}$: $y' = -(x/2)e^{-x^2/4}$, $y'' = (x^2/4 - 1/2)e^{-x^2/4}$, and

$$ \frac{(y')^2}{y} - \frac{y}{2} = \frac{(x^2/4)e^{-x^2/2}}{e^{-x^2/4}} - \frac{1}{2}e^{-x^2/4} = \Bigl(\frac{x^2}{4} - \frac12\Bigr)e^{-x^2/4} = y'' . $$

Initial conditions: $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 0$. First-integral check: $(y')^2/y^2 + \ln y = x^2/4 - x^2/4 = 0 = C$ along the selected solution. Validity: $y \gt 0$ for all $x$, so the equation is nonsingular on the solution; the general family with $A \lt 0$ solves the equation on $y \lt 0$ via the $\ln|y|$ form of the first integral.

Qualitative behavior

  • Equilibria: $y \equiv 0$ is a degenerate solution; $(y, y') = (0,0)$ is the only "rest point" of the equivalent system $y' = v$, $v' = v^2/y - y/2$, but the vector field is undefined at $y = 0$, so it is a degenerate point rather than a hyperbolic equilibrium.
  • Existence/uniqueness: for $y \neq 0$ the RHS is $C^\infty$; solutions with $y \neq 0$ never reach $y = 0$ in finite $x$ (they are Gaussians), and no $C^2$ solution can cross $y = 0$ with $y' \neq 0$ (then $y'' = (y')^2/y - y/2$ would blow up), so within $C^2$ solutions the zero solution is the only one passing through $y = 0$.
  • Shape: every nontrivial solution is a bell curve: maximum $|A|$ at $x = B$, inflection points at $x = B \pm \sqrt2$, symmetric about $x = B$.
  • Phase portrait: orbits are the loops $v^2 = y^2(C - \ln y)$, $0 \lt y \lt e^C$, closed through the degenerate point $(0,0)$; each loop is traversed exactly once as $x$ runs from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ (infinite-period loops) — the solutions are not periodic despite the closed-looking orbits.
  • Asymptotics: $y \to 0^+$ and $y' \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, faster than any exponential: the solution approaches the equilibrium $y = 0$ from above in both directions of $x$.
  • Maximal interval: $\mathbb{R}$.

Final answer

$$ y(x) = e^{-x^2/4}, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}. \qquad \text{General solution:}\quad y = A\,e^{-(x-B)^2/4},\ A \neq 0,\ \text{plus}\ y \equiv 0 $$

Problem 7 — A Parameterized Conic Family: Exactness with Degenerate Cases

Equation and conditions

First-order equation with real parameter $k$:

$$ y' = -\frac{y + kx}{x + ky}, \qquad y(1) = 0, $$

equivalently

$$ (kx + y)\,dx + (x + ky)\,dy = 0. $$

Analyze the cases $k = 0$, $0 \lt |k| \lt 1$, $k = \pm 1$, $|k| \gt 1$.

Domain

  • The solved form requires $x + ky \neq 0$.
  • $k = 1$: the equation reduces to $y' = -1$ away from the line $x + y = 0$; solution $y = 1 - x$ valid on $\mathbb{R}$.
  • $k = -1$: $y' = 1$ away from $x - y = 0$; solution $y = x - 1$ valid on $\mathbb{R}$.
  • $|k| \lt 1$: the solution is one branch of a hyperbola, valid on all of $\mathbb{R}$ (the branch avoids $x + ky = 0$).
  • $|k| \gt 1$: the solution is an arc of an ellipse between the two points where $x + ky = 0$ (vertical tangents), namely $x \in \bigl(-k/\sqrt{k^2-1},\ k/\sqrt{k^2-1}\bigr)$.
  • $k = 0$: $y \equiv 0$ (from the data); the general family is $xy = \text{const}$ on $x \neq 0$.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: exact equations $(M_y = N_x)$ with linear coefficients; homogeneous equations.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the parameter $k$ changes the topological type of the solution family — ellipses for $|k| \gt 1$, hyperbolas for $|k| \lt 1$, parallel lines at $k = \pm 1$, and the degenerate hyperbola pair at $k = 0$. The structural content is the conic classification and the two exceptional values where the equation degenerates into a trivial linear equation ($y' = \mp 1$), which must be detected and analyzed separately. The general solution $k(x^2+y^2) + 2xy = k$ is implicit and rigorous.
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: no affine change can remove the parameter — $k$ is the ratio of the eigenvalues of the quadratic form, an affine invariant of the conic family. The three regimes are not rescalings of one another: they are different conic types.

Anti-template audit: $v = y/x$ reduces it (routine homogeneity) — but this is the secondary method; the primary method is exactness in differential form, and the pedagogical point is the parameter-dependent conic structure and the degenerate cases. The equation is not separable, not Bernoulli, not Riccati, and not linear for general $k$.

Solution method 1

Exact differential and conic classification.

  1. With $M = kx + y$, $N = x + ky$: $M_y = 1 = N_x$, so the form is exact on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  2. Potential: $F_x = kx + y \Rightarrow F = kx^2/2 + xy + \varphi(y)$; then $F_y = x + \varphi'(y) = x + ky$, so $\varphi = ky^2/2$: $$ F(x,y) = \frac{k}{2}(x^2 + y^2) + xy = C . $$
  3. Apply $y(1) = 0$: $C = k/2$. Condition-selected solution: $$ kx^2 + 2xy + ky^2 = k . $$
  4. Case analysis (the quadratic form has eigenvalues $k \pm 1$):
    • $|k| \lt 1$, $k \neq 0$: the form is indefinite — a hyperbola through $(1,0)$ with asymptotes of slopes $\bigl(\pm\sqrt{(1+k)/(1-k)} - 1\bigr)/\bigl(1 \pm\sqrt{(1+k)/(1-k)}\bigr)$; the branch through $(1,0)$ is a graph over all of $\mathbb{R}$.
    • $k = 0$: $2xy = 0$; through $(1,0)$ this selects $y \equiv 0$.
    • $k = 1$: $(x+y)^2 = 1$, and through $(1,0)$: $y = 1 - x$.
    • $k = -1$: $-(x-y)^2 = -1$, and through $(1,0)$: $y = x - 1$.
    • $|k| \gt 1$: the form is positive definite (for $k\gt1$) or negative definite but level-set-consistent (for $k\lt-1$) — an ellipse $x^2 + y^2 + \tfrac{2}{k}xy = 1$ through $(1,0)$; the solution arc is the piece of the ellipse between its two intersections with $x + ky = 0$, i.e. between $y = \pm 1/\sqrt{k^2-1}$.

Solution method 2

Homogeneity substitution $v = y/x$.

  1. Set $y = vx$, $y' = v + xv'$: $$ v + xv' = -\frac{v + k}{1 + kv} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad xv' = -\frac{k + 2v + kv^2}{1 + kv} . $$
  2. Separate: $$ \frac{(1+kv)\,dv}{k + 2v + kv^2} = -\frac{dx}{x} . $$
  3. The numerator is half the derivative of the denominator: $\frac{d}{dv}(k + 2v + kv^2) = 2(1+kv)$, so $$ \frac12 \ln|k + 2v + kv^2| = -\ln|x| + C_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k + 2v + kv^2 = \frac{C}{x^2}. $$
  4. Undo the substitution: $kx^2 + 2xy + ky^2 = C$; with $y(1)=0$, $C = k$. The same conic family, obtained independently. The degenerate cases $k = \pm 1$ correspond to the linear numerator/denominator cancellation in the original equation; $v = -1/k$-type branches and $x = 0$ are handled by the implicit form.

Verification

Implicit differentiation of $kx^2 + 2xy + ky^2 = k$:

$$ 2kx + 2y + 2xy' + 2kyy' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y'(x + ky) = -(kx + y) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = -\frac{y + kx}{x + ky}, $$

the original equation. Exactness: $M_y = N_x = 1$ (checked in Method 1, step 1). Initial condition: at $(1,0)$, $k + 0 + 0 = k$. Case checks: $k=1$: $y = 1-x$ gives $y' = -1 = -(y+x)/(x+y)$; $k=-1$: $y = x-1$ gives $y' = 1$; $k=0$: $y \equiv 0$ gives $0 = -0/x$. Validity: the implicit solution is smooth on the stated domains, with vertical tangents exactly where $x + ky = 0$ (for $|k| \gt 1$, the two endpoints of the arc).

Qualitative behavior

  • Equilibria: none except the constant solution $y \equiv 0$ in the case $k = 0$ (with data $y(1)=0$); the origin is the center of the conic family, not a solution point.
  • Existence/uniqueness: for $k \neq \pm 1$, the RHS is $C^1$ near $(1,0)$ (denominator $1 \neq 0$), so the IVP is locally unique; at $k = \pm 1$ the equation degenerates to $y' = \mp 1$ with the obvious unique solutions.
  • Geometry: closed orbits (ellipses) for $|k| \gt 1$ — the solution arc is not closed as a graph because of the two vertical tangents; unbounded branches for $|k| \lt 1$ with linear asymptotes; straight-line solutions at $k = \pm 1$; the degenerate pair of lines at $k = 0$.
  • Maximal interval: $\mathbb{R}$ for $|k| \le 1$ (the hyperbola branch and the lines); $(-k/\sqrt{k^2-1},\ k/\sqrt{k^2-1})$ for $|k| \gt 1$.

Final answer

$$ kx^2 + 2xy + ky^2 = k, \quad k \in \mathbb{R}, \qquad \text{with } y(1) = 0, \quad \text{ellipses for } |k|\gt1,\ \text{hyperbolas for } |k|\lt1,\ \text{lines } y = 1-x\ (k=1),\ y = x-1\ (k=-1),\ y\equiv 0\ (k=0) $$

Problem 8 — An Implicit Solution via Reversing the Roles of $x$ and $y$

Equation and conditions

First-order equation:

$$ y' = \frac{y}{x + y\ln y}, \qquad y \gt 0, \qquad y(1) = 1. $$

Domain

  • $y \gt 0$ (for $\ln y$); $x + y\ln y \neq 0$ in the solved form.
  • The condition-selected solution has $x + y\ln y = y(1 + \ln y + (\ln y)^2/2) \gt 0$ everywhere on the solution, which is defined on the maximal interval $x \in (0, \infty)$, with $y \to 0^+$ as $x \to 0^+$ and $y \to \infty$ as $x \to \infty$.
  • $y \equiv 0$ is a formal solution for $x \neq 0$ (the equation degenerates there); the point $(0,0)$ is a singular point of the equation.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: first-order equations solved "for $x$ as a function of $y$" (the role-reversal trick).
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the equation is not separable, not homogeneous, not Bernoulli, and not linear in either variable in its given form, but reversing the roles of $x$ and $y$ turns it into the linear equation $dx/dy = x/y + \ln y$, whose solution $x = y\bigl(C + (\ln y)^2/2\bigr)$ is explicit in $x$ and genuinely implicit in $y$ (no elementary inverse exists). The logarithmic term is the structural feature that defeats all direct methods.
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: the term $y\ln y$ is scale-breaking — under $(x,y)\mapsto(\lambda x,\lambda y)$ it becomes $\lambda y\ln(\lambda y) = \lambda y\ln y + \lambda y\ln\lambda$, i.e. it is not a homogeneous term; a translation in $y$ destroys the $\ln$ structure entirely. The equation is not obtainable from a standard template by relettering.

Anti-template audit: $u = y/x$ fails (a $\ln y$ term survives); $u = 1/y$ fails ($u' = -u^2/(xu - \ln u)$, not separable or linear); $u = y^n$ fails; $u = ax+by$ fails (no such combination); log/exp substitutions do not decouple it; direct separation fails. The role reversal $x \leftrightarrow y$ is the key and is not on the routine-substitution list.

Solution method 1

Reverse the roles: solve for $x$ as a function of $y$.

  1. By the inverse-function rule, $$ \frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{1}{y'} = \frac{x + y\ln y}{y} = \frac{x}{y} + \ln y . $$
  2. This is a first-order linear equation in $x(y)$: $$ \frac{dx}{dy} - \frac{1}{y}\,x = \ln y . $$
  3. Integrating factor $\mu = e^{-\int dy/y} = 1/y$: $$ \frac{d}{dy}\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \frac{\ln y}{y} . $$
  4. Integrate (the right side is $\ln y\, d(\ln y)$): $$ \frac{x}{y} = \frac{(\ln y)^2}{2} + C . $$
  5. Undo the role reversal: the general solution is $$ x = y\left(C + \frac{(\ln y)^2}{2}\right). $$
  6. Apply $y(1) = 1$: $1 = 1\cdot(C + 0)$, so $C = 1$. Condition-selected solution:
    $$ x = y\left(1 + \frac{(\ln y)^2}{2}\right). $$

Solution method 2

Independent power-series (reversion) derivation about $x = 1$.

  1. Expand the condition-selected solution about $y = 1$: with $t = y - 1$, $$ \ln(1+t) = t - \frac{t^2}{2} + \frac{t^3}{3} - \frac{t^4}{4} + \frac{t^5}{5} - \cdots, \quad \frac{(\ln(1+t))^2}{2} = \frac{t^2}{2} - \frac{t^3}{2} + \frac{11}{24}t^4 - \frac{5}{12}t^5 + \cdots, $$ so $$ x = (1+t)\left(1 + \frac{(\ln(1+t))^2}{2}\right) = 1 + t + \frac{t^2}{2} + 0\cdot t^3 - \frac{1}{24}t^4 + \frac{1}{24}t^5 + O(t^6). $$
  2. Revert the series (Lagrange-style coefficient matching) to get $t = y - 1$ as a series in $s = x - 1$: $$ y = 1 + s - \frac{s^2}{2} + \frac{s^3}{2} - \frac{7}{12}s^4 + \frac{19}{24}s^5 + O(s^6). $$
  3. Independent derivation from the ODE itself (Taylor method). At $x = 1$: $y'(1) = 1/(1 + 0) = 1$. Differentiating the ODE, $$ y'' = \frac{y'(x + y\ln y) - y\bigl(1 + y'\ln y + y'\bigr)}{(x + y\ln y)^2}, $$ gives $y''(1) = [1\cdot 1 - 1(1+0+1)]/1 = -1$, so the coefficient of $s^2$ is $-1/2$. One more differentiation gives $y'''(1) = 3$ (computed from $y'' = N/D^2$ with $D = x + y\ln y$, $N = y'D - y(1 + y'\ln y + y')$: $D(1)=1$, $D'(1)=2$, $N(1)=-1$, $N'(1)=-1$, so $y'''(1) = (N'D - 2ND')/D^3 = -1 + 4 = 3$), hence the coefficient of $s^3$ is $1/2$.
  4. Both routes agree term by term: $$ y(x) = 1 + (x-1) - \frac{(x-1)^2}{2} + \frac{(x-1)^3}{2} + O\bigl((x-1)^4\bigr), $$ which is exactly the reversion of the implicit solution.

Verification

Differentiate the implicit solution with respect to $y$:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{d}{dy}\Bigl(y + \frac{y(\ln y)^2}{2}\Bigr) = 1 + \frac{(\ln y)^2}{2} + \ln y . $$

But $x/y + \ln y = 1 + (\ln y)^2/2 + \ln y$, so $dx/dy = x/y + \ln y = (x + y\ln y)/y$, hence

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{x + y\ln y}, $$

the original equation. Initial condition: at $(x,y) = (1,1)$, $1 = 1\cdot(1 + 0)$. Series check: the coefficients $1, -1/2, 1/2$ obtained by reversion equal those from the Taylor method. Validity: $dx/dy = ((1+\ln y)^2 + 1)/2 \gt 0$ for all $y \gt 0$, so $x(y)$ is strictly increasing — the inverse $y(x)$ exists on $x \in (0,\infty)$.

Qualitative behavior

  • Singular point: $(0,0)$ is a singular point of the equation; the solution enters it tangentially ($y'(x) \to 0^+$ as $x \to 0^+$, since $y' = 2/((1+\ln y)^2 + 1) \to 0$ as $y \to 0^+$).
  • Existence/uniqueness: the reversed equation is linear in $x$, so the solution through $(1,1)$ is unique; in the original variables the RHS is $C^1$ near $(1,1)$ ($x + y\ln y = 1 \neq 0$).
  • Monotonicity: $y' = y/(x + y\ln y) \gt 0$ on the solution — strictly increasing; no turning points.
  • Asymptotics: as $x \to 0^+$, $y \to 0^+$ (tangentially); as $x \to \infty$, $x \sim y(\ln y)^2/2$, so $y$ grows sublinearly, $y \sim 2x/(\ln x)^2$.
  • Maximal interval: $(0,\infty)$.

Final answer

$$ x = y\left(1 + \frac{(\ln y)^2}{2}\right), \qquad y \gt 0,\ x \in (0,\infty), \qquad\text{(implicit in } y,\ \text{no elementary inverse)} $$

Problem 9 — A Third-Order Equation with Exponential-Core Factorization

Equation and conditions

Third-order equation:

$$ y''' = \frac{(y'')^2}{y'}, \qquad y(0) = 0, \quad y'(0) = 1, \quad y''(0) = -1. $$

Domain

  • $y' \neq 0$ is required; the initial data satisfy $y'(0) = 1 \neq 0$.
  • The constant solutions $y \equiv c$ (with $y' \equiv 0$) are the degenerate solutions, where the equation is not defined as written but which are limits of the regular family.
  • The condition-selected solution $y = 1 - e^{-x}$ is defined for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, with $y' = e^{-x} \neq 0$.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: higher-order equations with a factorization structure, e.g. linear constant-coefficient equations written via characteristic polynomials; also the second-order class $u'' = (u')^2/u$.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the equation is nonlinear and third-order, yet it factorizes through a single logarithmic derivative: $y'''/y' - (y''/y')^2 = (\ln y')'' = 0$. Every regular solution is an affine image of an exponential core, $y = C_1 e^{ax} + C_2$, a two-mode family (constant mode + exponential mode) for a third-order equation. The coefficient structure $(y'')^2/y'$ is exactly the obstruction to $(\ln y')'$ being constant, which is the hidden factorization.
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: the equation is not linear (the quadratic term $(y'')^2$ with the $y'$-denominator is essential); multiplying by $y'$ or shifting $y$ does not linearize it; the structure is the logarithmic second derivative of $y'$, which is invariant under $y \mapsto ay + b$ (an invariance the family $y = C_1e^{ax}+C_2$ exhibits). No standard named equation matches it.

Anti-template audit: the routine first-order substitutions do not apply to a third-order equation; the standard reductions are the two used as methods below, and neither is a restatement of the other: Method 1 integrates the whole equation in one step via the invariant; Method 2 performs the classical double reduction of order. The equation is not a constant-coefficient linear equation, not an Euler equation, and not a standard "missing $y$" template such as $y''' = f(x)$.

Solution method 1

One-step invariant integration: $(\ln y')'' = 0$.

  1. Divide by $y'$ and recognize the logarithmic-derivative structure: $$ \frac{y'''}{y'} - \left(\frac{y''}{y'}\right)^2 = 0 . $$
  2. The left side is $(\ln y')''$: with $w = \ln y'$, $$ w'' = \left(\frac{y''}{y'}\right)' = \frac{y'''y' - (y'')^2}{(y')^2} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y''' = \frac{(y'')^2}{y'} . $$
  3. Integrate twice: $$ (\ln y')' = \frac{y''}{y'} = a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln y' = ax + b_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = e^{b_0} e^{ax}. $$
  4. Integrate once more: the general solution is $$ y = C_1 e^{ax} + C_2, \qquad C_1 \neq 0,\ a \in \mathbb{R}, $$ (three parameters: $a$, $C_1$, $C_2$ — correct for third order), plus the degenerate constants $y \equiv c$.
  5. Apply the conditions: $a = y''(0)/y'(0) = -1$; $y'(0) = C_1a e^0 = C_1(-1) = 1$, so $C_1 = -1$; $y(0) = C_1 + C_2 = 0$, so $C_2 = 1$. Condition-selected solution:
    $$ y = 1 - e^{-x}. $$

Solution method 2

Double reduction of order: $p = y'$, then $q(p) = p'$.

  1. First reduction: $p = y'$ gives $y''' = p''$, so $$ p'' = \frac{(p')^2}{p}, $$ a second-order equation for $p$ in which $x$ is missing.
  2. Second reduction: set $q(p) = p'$, so $p'' = q\,dq/dp$: $$ q\,\frac{dq}{dp} = \frac{q^2}{p} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dq}{q} = \frac{dp}{p} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q = ap . $$
  3. Undo the second reduction: $p' = ap \Rightarrow p = be^{ax}$.
  4. Undo the first reduction: $y' = be^{ax} \Rightarrow y = (b/a)e^{ax} + C$, i.e. $y = C_1e^{ax} + C_2$ — the same family, here obtained through the classical chain of reductions rather than the single invariant step.
  5. Conditions as in Method 1: $a = -1$, $b = 1$, $C = 1$: $y = 1 - e^{-x}$.

Verification

For $y = 1 - e^{-x}$: $y' = e^{-x}$, $y'' = -e^{-x}$, $y''' = e^{-x}$, and

$$ \frac{(y'')^2}{y'} = \frac{e^{-2x}}{e^{-x}} = e^{-x} = y'''. $$

Initial conditions: $y(0) = 0$, $y'(0) = 1$, $y''(0) = -1$. General family check: $y = C_1e^{ax} + C_2$ has $y' = C_1ae^{ax}$, $y'' = C_1a^2e^{ax}$, $y''' = C_1a^3e^{ax}$, and $(y'')^2/y' = C_1^2a^4e^{2ax}/(C_1ae^{ax}) = C_1a^3e^{ax}$. Validity: $y' = e^{-x} \neq 0$ for all $x$, so the solution is regular on $\mathbb{R}$.

Qualitative behavior

  • Degenerate solutions: the constants $y \equiv c$ (where $y' \equiv 0$) are the only solutions excluded from the regular family; the equation is undefined on them as written, but they are uniform limits of regular solutions (e.g. $C_1 \to 0$ with $C_1a$ fixed).
  • Existence/uniqueness: near the initial data $y' \neq 0$, the RHS $(y'')^2/y'$ is $C^\infty$, so the third-order IVP has a unique local solution; the solution extends to $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Behavior of the selected solution: $y' = e^{-x} \gt 0$ (strictly increasing), $y'' = -e^{-x} \lt 0$ (concave), monotone exponential relaxation to the limit $y \to 1^-$ as $x \to \infty$, with $y \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$; no oscillation, no turning points (for this IVP).
  • General family: exponential growth ($a\gt0$) or decay ($a\lt0$) on top of a constant level $C_2$; the family is invariant under $y \mapsto \alpha y + \beta$, reflecting the invariance of the equation.
  • Maximal interval: $\mathbb{R}$ for every regular solution.

Final answer

$$ y(x) = 1 - e^{-x}, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}. \qquad \text{General solution:}\quad y = C_1e^{ax} + C_2,\ C_1 \neq 0,\ \text{plus}\ y \equiv \text{const} $$

Problem 10 — The Exponential-Integral Inverse: A Series-and-Special-Function Solution

Equation and conditions

First-order equation:

$$ (1 - xy)\,y' = y, \qquad y(0) = 1. $$

Domain

  • The solved form $y' = y/(1 - xy)$ requires $1 - xy \neq 0$; the IVP data satisfy $1 - 0\cdot 1 = 1 \neq 0$.
  • The condition-selected solution has $y \gt 0$, $xy \lt 1$ on its branch; it is defined on the maximal interval $x \in (-\infty, x^\ast)$, where $x^\ast$ (the first point where $xy = 1$ along the solution curve, i.e. the first branch point of the inverse function) satisfies $x^\ast \approx 0.427$. The curve $x = e^{-y}(\operatorname{Ei}(y) - \operatorname{Ei}(1))$, $y \in (0,\infty)$, is the maximal solution curve; the IVP selects its first branch.

Originality note

Originality note: This is designed as a structurally non-routine problem rather than a cosmetic variation of a standard textbook equation. Absolute literature-wide uniqueness cannot be guaranteed.

  • Nearest familiar equation class: equations solved by swapping dependent and independent variable; Riccati-like multiplicative couplings of $x$ and $y$.
  • Why this is structurally non-routine: the equation is not separable (the coupling $1 - xy$ involves both variables), not homogeneous, not Bernoulli, and not Riccati; the role reversal gives the linear equation $dx/dy = 1/y - x$, whose solution requires the exponential integral $\operatorname{Ei}$ — a named special function that is warranted here because it is the exact, rigorous closed form, not decoration. The solution is an inverse-function pair, and the power series in $x$ has a clean recurrence and a finite radius of convergence governed by a branch point, both of which are demonstrable independently.
  • Why it is not merely a rescaling, translation, or constant-modified textbook exercise: translating $x$ or $y$ destroys the multiplicative structure $1 - xy$ (the constant term is pinned by the requirement that the swapped equation be solvable in closed form via Ei); scaling $x, y$ changes the location of the singularity $xy = 1$ and of the initial data nontrivially. The Ei-structure, not the constants, is the content.

Anti-template audit: $u = y/x$ fails; $u = 1/y$ fails; $u = xy$ fails (unlike Problem 2, here $u = xy$ does not eliminate $x$: $xu' = u(1-u) + ...$ check: with $u = xy$, $y' = (u'x - u)/x^2$ and the equation becomes $xu' - u = u/(1-u)\cdot x...$ — it retains $x$ nontrivially, so no separation); log/exp substitutions fail; the equation is not linear in $y$. The role reversal is the key step and yields a linear equation.

Solution method 1

Role reversal and the exponential integral.

  1. Treat $x$ as a function of $y$ (the solution satisfies $y'(0) = 1/(1-0) = 1 \neq 0$, so the inverse exists locally): $$ \frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{1 - xy}{y} = \frac{1}{y} - x . $$
  2. This is a first-order linear equation: $$ \frac{dx}{dy} + x = \frac{1}{y}. $$
  3. Integrating factor $e^y$: $$ \frac{d}{dy}\bigl(x\,e^{y}\bigr) = \frac{e^{y}}{y} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x\,e^{y} = \operatorname{Ei}(y) + C, \qquad \operatorname{Ei}(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{y} \frac{e^{t}}{t}\,dt . $$
  4. Apply the condition in swapped form $(x,y) = (0,1)$: $0 = \operatorname{Ei}(1) + C$, so $C = -\operatorname{Ei}(1)$. Condition-selected solution:
    $$ x = e^{-y}\bigl(\operatorname{Ei}(y) - \operatorname{Ei}(1)\bigr). $$
  5. This defines $y(x)$ implicitly (no elementary inverse); the power series of $y$ about $x = 0$ is given in Method 2.

Solution method 2

Power series with recurrence, cross-checked by Lagrange reversion of the Ei-solution.

  1. Substitute $y = \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n x^n$, $a_0 = 1$, into $(1-xy)y' = y$, i.e. $y' = y + xyy'$. With $c_n = [x^n](y\cdot y') = \sum_{i+j=n} a_i\,(j+1)a_{j+1}$, matching coefficients gives the recurrence $$ (n+1)a_{n+1} = a_n + c_{n-1}, \qquad c_{-1} = 0. $$
  2. Compute: $$ a_0 = 1,\quad a_1 = 1,\quad a_2 = 1,\quad a_3 = \frac{4}{3},\quad a_4 = \frac{25}{12},\quad a_5 = \frac{71}{20}, $$ so $$ y = 1 + x + x^2 + \frac43 x^3 + \frac{25}{12}x^4 + \frac{71}{20}x^5 + O(x^6). $$
  3. Independent cross-check by Lagrange reversion of the exact solution. Expand $\operatorname{Ei}(1+t) = \operatorname{Ei}(1) + e\bigl[t + t^3/6 - t^4/12 + (3/40)t^5 + \cdots\bigr]$ (from $\operatorname{Ei}' = e^y/y$, successive derivatives at $y=1$: $e, 0, e, -2e, 9e$), so with $t = y - 1$, $$ x = e^{-t}\Bigl[t + \frac{t^3}{6} - \frac{t^4}{12} + \frac{3}{40}t^5 + \cdots\Bigr] = t - t^2 + \frac23 t^3 - \frac{5}{12}t^4 + \frac{17}{60}t^5 + \cdots $$
  4. Reverting this series gives $$ t = x + x^2 + \frac43 x^3 + \frac{25}{12}x^4 + \frac{71}{20}x^5 + \cdots, $$ in exact agreement with the recurrence (the reversion of the first four terms checks $a_2, a_3, a_4$; the fifth term checks $a_5 = 71/20$).
  5. Radius of convergence: $y(x)$ is the inverse of $x(y)$; its singularities lie over the critical values of $x(y)$. Since $dx/dy = 1/y - x = (1-xy)/y$, the critical points satisfy $xy = 1$, i.e. $y\,e^{-y}(\operatorname{Ei}(y)-\operatorname{Ei}(1)) = 1$ for $y \gt 1$. This has a first solution at $y^\ast \approx 2.35$, giving the branch point $x^\ast = 1/y^\ast \approx 0.427$, so the series has radius of convergence $R = x^\ast \approx 0.427$ — finite, and governed by the vertical tangent of $y(x)$, a qualitative insight the numerical value serves to make concrete.

Verification

Differentiate the exact solution:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = -e^{-y}\bigl(\operatorname{Ei}(y) - \operatorname{Ei}(1)\bigr) + e^{-y}\cdot\frac{e^{y}}{y} = \frac{1}{y} - x, $$

so $dy/dx = 1/(1/y - x) = y/(1 - xy)$, i.e. $(1-xy)y' = y$. Initial condition: at $(x,y) = (0,1)$, $0 = e^{-1}(\operatorname{Ei}(1) - \operatorname{Ei}(1)) = 0$. Series verification by direct substitution: with $y = 1 + x + x^2 + (4/3)x^3 + (25/12)x^4 + \cdots$ and $y' = 1 + 2x + 4x^2 + (25/3)x^3 + \cdots$, the product $(1 - x - x^2 - x^3 - \cdots)(1 + 2x + 4x^2 + (25/3)x^3 + \cdots)$ has coefficients $1, 1, 1, 4/3$ (e.g. $[x^3] = 25/3 - 4 - 2 - 1 = 4/3$), matching $a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3$. Validity: on the stated branch, $y \gt 0$ and $1 - xy \gt 0$, so the solved form is regular there.

Qualitative behavior

  • Singular structure: $y \equiv 0$ is a formal solution ($y' = 0 = 0/(1-0)$) but not in the domain of interest; the curve $1 - xy = 0$ is the singular set, hit by the solution at the branch point $x^\ast \approx 0.427$.
