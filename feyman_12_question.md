
Feynman did **not** leave behind an authoritative published list of exactly 12 questions. The famous idea is that he advised keeping “a dozen of your favorite problems” continuously in mind, then testing every new fact, technique, or result against them for a possible connection. [nesslabs](https://nesslabs.com/favorite-problems)

## The original idea

The quotation is generally attributed to mathematician Gian-Carlo Rota’s recollection of Feynman’s advice:

> “You have to keep a dozen of your favorite problems constantly present in your mind… Every time you hear or read a new trick or a new result, test it against each of your twelve problems to see whether it helps.”

The point is not to maintain a to-do list, nor to select 12 goals to complete this quarter. Instead, they are durable, open-ended questions that remain intellectually productive over years. [nesslabs](https://nesslabs.com/favorite-problems)

## Were Feynman’s actual 12 known?

No reliable primary source establishes **the** canonical twelve. Lists online that present twelve questions as “Feynman’s” are typically reconstructions: a mixture of documented interests, episodes from his life, and speculative examples.

For instance, one popular reconstruction includes problems related to:

1. Estimating the probability of premature nuclear detonation.
2. Mentally tracking time accurately.
3. Building large computers from basic components.
4. Learning precise Chinese handwriting.
5. Understanding the unity of light, radio, magnetism, and electricity.
6. Performing two-handed drum polyrhythms.
7. Teaching introductory physics effectively.
8. Finding the smallest functional machine.
9. Calculating light emission by excited atoms.
10. Investigating the Challenger disaster.
11. Applying nuclear physics toward peace rather than war.
12. Continuing meaningful research despite fame.

But this should be treated as a **modern illustrative list**, not a verified historical document written by Feynman. [fortelabs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/12-favorite-problems-how-to-spark-genius-with-the-power-of-open-questions/)

## What makes a good “Feynman problem”

A useful favorite problem is:

- **Open-ended:** it cannot be finished in a weekend.
- **Personal:** you genuinely return to it without external pressure.
- **Specific enough to test:** a new paper, tool, proof, or conversation might clearly bear on it.
- **Generative:** it produces smaller experiments, projects, or research questions.
- **Stable but revisable:** keep it long enough for serendipitous connections, but replace questions that no longer matter.

A poor version is: “Become better at security.”

A stronger version is: “How can we make authorization policies formally reviewable, testable, and explainable across heterogeneous cloud and application systems?”

## Example set for you

Given your interests in cloud platforms, security, identity, distributed systems, mathematics, and AI-assisted engineering, a tailored set could be:

1. How can a multi-cloud control plane represent compute, identity, network, cost, and policy without erasing each provider’s important differences?
2. What minimal authorization model can express real production relationships while remaining auditable and explainable to operators?
3. How can policy changes be simulated, verified, and rolled back with the same rigor as infrastructure changes?
4. Which invariants make distributed infrastructure workflows safe under retries, partial failure, concurrency, and eventual consistency?
5. How can one measure and continuously reduce privilege drift across cloud IAM, Kubernetes RBAC, SaaS, and application authorization?
6. What would a practical, reproducible vulnerability-research lab look like for browser/runtime and cloud-control-plane targets?
7. How can fuzzing harnesses be designed to discover semantic bugs rather than merely increase code coverage?
8. How can AI coding agents be bounded so that they are useful in infrastructure repositories without gaining unsafe authority?
9. What architecture makes API-driven provisioning observable, idempotent, cost-aware, and portable?
10. Which mathematical structures best clarify access-control graphs, distributed consistency, or security-policy composition?
11. How can formal methods be introduced into day-to-day platform engineering without creating excessive process overhead?
12. What important engineering problem becomes much easier if identity, policy, workflow state, and audit evidence are modeled as one coherent system?

## How to use them

Keep the questions in a short note that you revisit occasionally. When you encounter a new paper, CVE, Terraform provider behavior, OpenFGA modeling pattern, protocol, theorem, or AI-agent technique, ask:

\[
\text{“Does this change how I think about any one of my twelve problems?”}
\]

Most inputs will not. A few will create unexpected links—and that compounding process is the practical insight behind the “12 favorite problems” method. [nesslabs](https://nesslabs.com/favorite-problems)
