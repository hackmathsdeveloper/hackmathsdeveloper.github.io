
Book I of Euclid’s *Elements* contains **48 propositions**: both theorems and geometric construction problems. Below is the complete list in the original order, paraphrased in modern mathematical language. Book I also begins with 23 definitions, 5 postulates, and 5 common notions; these are foundations rather than proved results. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

In the statements below:
- A “segment” means a finite straight line.
- Two right angles equal \(180^\circ\).
- “Equal” plane figures means equal in area, not necessarily congruent.
- Figures “between the same parallels” have their bases on one of two parallel lines and their opposite vertices or sides on the other. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

## 1. Basic constructions and triangle congruence: I.1–I.12

1. I.1 — Equilateral triangle construction. Construct an equilateral triangle on a given segment.

2. I.2 — Copying a segment to a given point. Starting at a given point, construct a segment equal in length to a given segment.

3. I.3 — Cutting off a given length. Given two unequal segments, cut from the longer a segment equal to the shorter.

4. I.4 — Side–angle–side congruence (SAS). If two triangles have two corresponding sides equal and their included angles equal, their remaining sides and corresponding angles are equal, and the triangles are equal.

5. I.5 — Base angles of an isosceles triangle. The base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal. If the equal sides are extended beyond the base, the exterior angles beneath the base are also equal.

6. I.6 — Converse of the isosceles triangle theorem. If two angles of a triangle are equal, the sides opposite them are equal. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

7. I.7 — Uniqueness of a triangle on a fixed side of its base. Given a segment \(AB\), there cannot be two distinct points \(C,D\) on the same side of \(AB\) satisfying both \(AC=AD\) and \(BC=BD\).

8. I.8 — Side–side–side congruence (SSS). If the three sides of one triangle equal the corresponding three sides of another, the corresponding angles are equal.

9. I.9 — Angle bisection. Construct the bisector of a given angle.

10. I.10 — Segment bisection. Construct the midpoint of a given segment.

11. I.11 — Perpendicular at a point on a line. Construct a perpendicular to a given line through a given point on that line.

12. I.12 — Perpendicular from a point outside a line. Construct a perpendicular to a given line from a point not on it. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

## 2. Angles, inequalities, and further triangle constructions: I.13–I.26

13. I.13 — Angles on a straight line. A ray standing on a straight line makes adjacent angles whose sum is \(180^\circ\).

14. I.14 — Converse of the straight-angle property. If two rays on opposite sides of a third ray make adjacent angles summing to \(180^\circ\), those two rays form a straight line.

15. I.15 — Vertically opposite angles. When two straight lines intersect, their vertically opposite angles are equal.  
    Corollary: the angles around the intersection sum to \(360^\circ\).

16. I.16 — Exterior-angle inequality. An exterior angle of a triangle is greater than either nonadjacent interior angle.

17. I.17 — Sum of two triangle angles. Any two interior angles of a triangle sum to less than \(180^\circ\).

18. I.18 — Greater side, greater opposite angle. In a triangle, the greater side lies opposite the greater angle.

19. I.19 — Greater angle, greater opposite side. In a triangle, the greater angle lies opposite the greater side.

20. I.20 — Triangle inequality. The sum of any two sides of a triangle exceeds the third side. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

21. I.21 — Segments drawn to an interior point. If \(D\) lies inside triangle \(ABC\), then
    \[
    BD+DC<BA+AC,
    \qquad
    \angle BDC>\angle BAC.
    \]

22. I.22 — Triangle construction from three lengths. Construct a triangle with three prescribed side lengths, provided the sum of any two exceeds the third.

23. I.23 — Copying an angle. At a given point on a given line, construct an angle equal to a given angle.

24. I.24 — Hinge theorem. If two triangles have two corresponding sides equal, the triangle with the greater included angle has the greater third side.

25. I.25 — Converse of the hinge theorem. If two triangles have two corresponding sides equal, the triangle with the greater third side has the greater included angle.

26. I.26 — Angle–side congruence (ASA and AAS). If two triangles have two corresponding angles equal and one corresponding side equal—either the included side or a side opposite one of the equal angles—the remaining sides and angle are equal. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

## 3. Parallel lines and parallelograms: I.27–I.34

27. I.27 — Alternate-angle criterion for parallelism. If a transversal makes equal alternate interior angles with two lines, those lines are parallel.

28. I.28 — Two further criteria for parallelism. Two lines are parallel if a transversal makes either:
    - equal corresponding angles; or
    - interior angles on the same side summing to \(180^\circ\).

29. I.29 — Angles made by a transversal of parallel lines. A transversal crossing parallel lines produces:
    - equal alternate interior angles;
    - equal corresponding angles;
    - interior angles on the same side summing to \(180^\circ\).

30. I.30 — Lines parallel to the same line. Distinct lines parallel to the same line are parallel to one another.

31. I.31 — Parallel-line construction. Through a given point, construct a straight line parallel to a given straight line. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

32. I.32 — Exterior-angle theorem and triangle angle sum. An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two nonadjacent interior angles. Consequently,
    \[
    \angle A+\angle B+\angle C=180^\circ.
    \]

33. I.33 — Joining corresponding endpoints of equal parallel segments. The segments joining the corresponding endpoints of two equal, parallel segments, taken in the same direction, are themselves equal and parallel.

34. I.34 — Properties of a parallelogram. In a parallelogram:
    - opposite sides are equal;
    - opposite angles are equal;
    - a diagonal divides the figure into two equal-area triangles. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

## 4. Areas and equal-area constructions: I.35–I.45

35. I.35 — Parallelograms on the same base. Parallelograms on the same base and between the same parallels have equal areas.

36. I.36 — Parallelograms on equal bases. Parallelograms on equal bases and between the same parallels have equal areas.

37. I.37 — Triangles on the same base. Triangles on the same base and between the same parallels have equal areas.

38. I.38 — Triangles on equal bases. Triangles on equal bases and between the same parallels have equal areas.

39. I.39 — Converse for triangles on the same base. Equal-area triangles on the same base and on the same side of it lie between the same parallels: the line joining their opposite vertices is parallel to the base.

40. I.40 — Converse for triangles on equal bases. Equal-area triangles on equal bases lying on the same straight line, with their opposite vertices on the same side, lie between the same parallels. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

41. I.41 — Parallelogram–triangle area comparison. A parallelogram and a triangle on the same base and between the same parallels satisfy
    \[
    \operatorname{Area}(\text{parallelogram})
    =2\operatorname{Area}(\text{triangle}).
    \]

42. I.42 — Parallelogram equal in area to a triangle. Construct a parallelogram equal in area to a given triangle, with an angle equal to a given angle.

43. I.43 — Complements about a diagonal. Draw two lines through a point on a parallelogram’s diagonal, each parallel to a pair of opposite sides. Of the four smaller parallelograms formed, the two not traversed by the original diagonal have equal areas.

44. I.44 — Applying a prescribed area to a given segment. Construct a parallelogram on a given segment, with a given angle, equal in area to a given triangle.

45. I.45 — Parallelogram equal in area to a rectilinear figure. Construct a parallelogram equal in area to a given polygonal figure, with an angle equal to a given angle. [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)

## 5. Squares and the Pythagorean theorem: I.46–I.48

46. I.46 — Square construction. Construct a square on a given segment.

47. I.47 — Pythagorean theorem. In a right triangle, the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides. In modern notation, if the legs have lengths \(a,b\) and the hypotenuse has length \(c\),
    \[
    c^2=a^2+b^2.
    \]

48. I.48 — Converse of the Pythagorean theorem. If the square on one side of a triangle equals the sum of the squares on its other two sides, the angle opposite the first side is a right angle:
    \[
    c^2=a^2+b^2
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \text{the angle opposite }c\text{ is }90^\circ.
    \]  [aleph0.clarku](http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/bookI.html)
