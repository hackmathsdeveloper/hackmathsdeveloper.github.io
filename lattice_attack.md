
A **lattice** attack turns algebraic relations involving a secret into a geometric problem: construct a lattice in which the secret corresponds to an unusually short vector, or to a lattice point unusually close to a known target, then use lattice-reduction algorithms to find it. It is a family of techniques, not one specific attack. A particularly important application is recovering an ECDSA private key when signing nonces are biased or partially leaked; I’ll use that to explain the mechanism. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

## 1. Why geometry helps solve equations

A lattice generated by linearly independent vectors \(b_1,\ldots,b_m\) is the set

\[
\mathcal L=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_jb_j:z_j\in\mathbb Z\right\}.
\]

The integer coefficients are crucial: a lattice is a discrete set of points, not the whole vector space. Different bases can describe exactly the same lattice. Lattice reduction replaces an awkward basis with one whose vectors are shorter and more nearly orthogonal, making special short vectors easier to identify. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

Two problems commonly used in cryptanalysis are:

- Shortest Vector Problem, or SVP: find a shortest nonzero lattice vector, or a sufficiently short approximation.
- Closest Vector Problem, or CVP: find a lattice point closest to a specified target, or a sufficiently good approximation. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

The cryptographic insight is to arrange the equations so that the correct secret produces a small residual, while incorrect guesses generally produce large residuals. Geometry then provides a way to exploit all those constraints together. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

## 2. ECDSA: where the exploitable equations come from

For an ECDSA signature \((r_i,s_i)\), write:

- \(n\): the elliptic-curve subgroup order.
- \(d\): the private key.
- \(h_i\): the message-hash representative.
- \(k_i\): the secret nonce for that signature.

The signing equation is

\[
s_i\equiv k_i^{-1}(h_i+r_i d)\pmod n.
\]

Rearranging gives

\[
k_i\equiv a_i d+b_i\pmod n,
\qquad
a_i=r_i s_i^{-1}\pmod n,
\qquad
b_i=h_i s_i^{-1}\pmod n.
\]

The attacker knows \(a_i\) and \(b_i\). Every signature therefore supplies a modular linear equation involving the same unknown \(d\), but a different unknown \(k_i\). Partial knowledge of those nonces turns these equations into an instance of the Hidden Number Problem. [forum.vac](https://forum.vac.dev/t/lattice-attacks-on-ecdsa/136)

With unconstrained nonces, these equations do not provide the small-error structure needed by this attack. The weakness appears when the attacker can bound some unknown part of each nonce—for example, because its leading bits are known or because the nonce is unusually small. [kudelskisecurity](https://kudelskisecurity.com/research/lattice-free-half-half-attack-on-bitcoin-and-ethereum)

## 3. Turn partial nonce knowledge into small errors

Suppose the attacker has an approximation \(\widehat{k}_i\) such that

\[
k_i=\widehat{k}_i+e_i,
\qquad |e_i|\le B.
\]

Substituting into the signature relation yields

\[
a_i d+b_i-\widehat{k}_i\equiv e_i\pmod n.
\]

Define the known quantity

\[
c_i=\widehat{k}_i-b_i.
\]

Then, for some integer \(z_i\),

\[
a_i d-c_i-nz_i=e_i.
\]

The important change is that the unknowns now satisfy integer equations with small errors. One signature leaves many possibilities, but many signatures constrain the same \(d\) simultaneously. This is the structure exploited by lattice-based Hidden Number Problem attacks. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

## 4. A concrete lattice construction

One illustrative CVP construction uses the following row basis for \(m\) signatures:

\[
\mathbf M=
\begin{pmatrix}
n&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&n&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&n&0\\
a_1&a_2&\cdots&a_m&B/n
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Choose the target

\[
t=(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_m,0).
\]

Taking integer coefficients \((-z_1,\ldots,-z_m,d)\) produces the lattice point

\[
v=(a_1d-nz_1,\ldots,a_md-nz_m,dB/n).
\]

Consequently,

\[
v-t=(e_1,\ldots,e_m,dB/n).
\]

For the representative \(0\le d<n\), every coordinate of this difference has magnitude at most \(B\). Thus the correct key gives a lattice point near the target. The factor \(B/n\) balances the private-key coordinate against the nonce-error coordinates; rational entries can be cleared by scaling the entire construction. This illustrates the standard transformation from small modular errors to a closest-vector problem. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

The attack then reduces the basis and searches for a sufficiently close point, potentially using an embedding that converts the problem into a short-vector search. Reduction is a computational aid—not a guarantee that the correct point will be found. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

## 5. What determines whether it succeeds?

The main trade-offs are:

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| More accurate nonce information | Smaller errors make the correct solution easier to distinguish. |
| More useful signatures | More constraints help identify the key, but also increase lattice dimension. |
| Stronger lattice reduction | Can solve harder instances, at greater computational cost. |
| Incorrect or noisy leakage assumptions | May prevent recovery or require a more sophisticated model. |

There is no universal “number of signatures required.” It depends on the leakage pattern, error bounds, lattice construction, and reduction method. Even closely related nonce weaknesses can require substantially different attacks. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

LLL is a polynomial-time basis-reduction algorithm that finds relatively short vectors, not an exact general-purpose SVP solver. A lattice attack succeeds when the constructed instance has enough exploitable structure for the available reduction and search methods. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)

## 6. What this does—and does not—break

This ECDSA attack exploits nonce generation or nonce leakage, rather than directly solving the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem. Nonce reuse is an even simpler failure: reusing a nonce can permit algebraic key recovery without lattice machinery. Lattice methods matter when the attacker has weaker information distributed across multiple signatures. [forum.vac](https://forum.vac.dev/t/lattice-attacks-on-ecdsa/136)

The broader technique also applies to other constructions: small-root and knapsack attacks use lattice reduction, while attacks on lattice-based cryptography may seek short vectors or decode noisy linear equations. In the latter setting, those geometric problems are deliberately made difficult through parameter selection—not automatically easy just because they can be expressed as lattices. [eprint.iacr](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/032.pdf)
