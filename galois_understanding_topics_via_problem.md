
Below is a problem-driven syllabus with **100 problems**: ten for each of the ten topic groups in the earlier roadmap.

Each problem includes:
- A task to attempt.
- An answer checkpoint or proof direction—not a full solution.
- A comment explaining what the problem reveals and why the theory becomes useful.

These are selected and formulated as a coherent learning sequence, not copied from one problem sheet. The scope follows standard courses and exercise collections: field extensions, the Galois correspondence, radical solvability, finite fields, and classical applications. [math.iitb.ac](https://www.math.iitb.ac.in/~jkv/algebra2/prob.pdf)

Throughout, \(\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}\), and field degrees are over the indicated base field.

## 1. Prerequisites: groups, polynomials, and linear algebra

### Motivation

Before asking “Can I solve this equation?”, ask two simpler questions: “What algebraic relations constrain its roots?” and “Which permutations preserve those relations?” Polynomial algebra answers the first; group theory provides the language for the second. These are the standard prerequisites for a first Galois-theory course. [webhomes.maths.ed.ac](https://webhomes.maths.ed.ac.uk/~tl/galois/)

1. Find every subgroup of \(S_3\), and determine which are normal.  
   Answer checkpoint: the trivial subgroup, three order-two subgroups, \(A_3\), and \(S_3\); only the trivial subgroup, \(A_3\), and \(S_3\) are normal.  
   Why it matters: this becomes the complete intermediate-field classification for a typical irreducible cubic with Galois group \(S_3\).

2. Compute the multiplication table of \(C_2\times C_2\).  
   Answer checkpoint: there are three nonidentity elements, all of order two.  
   Why it matters: independent sign changes of two square roots produce exactly this group.

3. Prove the orbit–stabilizer theorem, then apply it to the natural action of \(S_3\) on three letters.  
   Answer checkpoint: each orbit has size three and each point stabilizer has order two.  
   Why it matters: later, root orbits correspond to conjugates, and stabilizers correspond to fields generated by roots.

4. Prove that \(x^3-2\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) in two ways.  
   Answer checkpoint: use the rational-root test and Eisenstein’s criterion at \(2\).  
   Why it matters: irreducibility determines the degree of a root and forces a transitive action on the roots.

5. Prove that \(x^4+1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: substitute \(x+1\) and apply Eisenstein at \(2\).  
   Why it matters: a polynomial can be irreducible without looking like a standard Eisenstein example.

6. Factor \(x^4-1\) over \(\mathbb Q\), \(\mathbb R\), and \(\mathbb C\).  
   Answer checkpoint: over \(\mathbb Q\), the irreducible factors are \(x-1\), \(x+1\), and \(x^2+1\); over \(\mathbb C\), all factors are linear.  
   Why it matters: factorization is always relative to a base field.

7. Show that \(K[x]/(f)\) is a field exactly when \(f\) is irreducible, for nonconstant \(f\in K[x]\).  
   Answer checkpoint: connect irreducibility to maximal ideals, or use Bézout’s identity to construct inverses.  
   Why it matters: this explains how “adjoin a root” becomes a rigorous field construction.

8. Find the inverse of \(1+\sqrt2\) inside \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   (1+\sqrt2)^{-1}=\sqrt2-1.
   \]
   Why it matters: adjoining a root does not merely provide one new number; it provides a field closed under all field operations.

9. Prove that \(1,\sqrt2\) are linearly independent over \(\mathbb Q\), but dependent over \(\mathbb R\).  
   Answer checkpoint: use the irrationality of \(\sqrt2\) for the first assertion.  
   Why it matters: field-extension degree is vector-space dimension, and dimension depends on the scalar field.

10. Compute the quotient \(S_3/A_3\).  
    Answer checkpoint: it is \(C_2\), with the sign homomorphism giving the quotient map.  
    Why it matters: this quotient later describes the quadratic subfield of an \(S_3\)-extension.

## 2. Field extensions and minimal polynomials

### Motivation

An equation gives you a root, but how much new arithmetic does that root introduce? Minimal polynomials and extension degrees measure this precisely. The central move is to replace manipulation of expressions by vector-space calculations. [webhomes.maths.ed.ac](https://webhomes.maths.ed.ac.uk/~tl/galois/)

1. Find the minimal polynomial of \(\sqrt2\) over \(\mathbb Q\) and over \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(x^2-2\) over the first field, \(x-\sqrt2\) over the second.  
   Implication: minimal polynomials are relative to the base field.

2. Find a basis of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2})/\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   1,\alpha,\alpha^2,\qquad \alpha=\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2}.
   \]
   Implication: every rational expression in \(\alpha\) reduces to a polynomial of degree at most two.

3. Find the minimal polynomial of \(\sqrt2+\sqrt3\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   x^4-10x^2+1.
   \]
   Prove that the element generates \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\) to establish degree four.  
   Implication: one element can encode an extension initially presented with two generators.

4. Show that \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\).  
   Answer checkpoint: if \(u=\sqrt2+\sqrt3\), then \(u^{-1}=\sqrt3-\sqrt2\). Recover both square roots from \(u\) and \(u^{-1}\).  
   Implication: the displayed generators of a field need not be minimal.

5. Compute \([\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3):\mathbb Q]\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(4\). Prove \(\sqrt3\notin\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\).  
   Implication: adjoining a second element increases the degree only if it is genuinely new.

6. Compute \([\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt8):\mathbb Q]\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(2\), because \(\sqrt8=2\sqrt2\).  
   Implication: blindly multiplying the degrees of displayed radicals gives wrong answers.

7. Prove the tower law using products of bases.  
   Answer checkpoint: if \((u_i)\) is a basis of \(L/E\) and \((v_j)\) a basis of \(E/K\), then \((u_iv_j)\) is a basis of \(L/K\).  
   Implication: degree arithmetic reflects an actual basis construction.

8. Prove that every element of a finite extension \(L/K\) is algebraic over \(K\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{[L:K]}\) must be linearly dependent.  
   Implication: finite-dimensional linear algebra produces polynomial relations.

9. Prove that if \(\alpha\) is algebraic over \(K\), then \(K[\alpha]=K(\alpha)\).  
   Answer checkpoint: use the irreducible minimal polynomial and Bézout’s identity to invert nonzero elements.  
   Implication: denominators introduce no further elements in a simple algebraic extension.

10. Show that if \(t\) is transcendental over \(K\), then \(K(t)/K\) has infinite degree.  
    Answer checkpoint: \(1,t,t^2,\ldots\) are linearly independent.  
    Implication: the finite-extension methods of elementary Galois theory do not apply indiscriminately to transcendental extensions.

## 3. Splitting fields, embeddings, and automorphisms

### Motivation

Knowing one root is often insufficient. The next problem is to construct a field containing all roots and determine which root substitutions extend to legitimate field maps. The extension of embeddings is a key tool behind this transition. [pub.math.leidenuniv](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~stevenhagenp/GaloisTheory.pdf)

1. Find the splitting field of \(x^2-2\) over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\), of degree two.  
   Implication: for a quadratic, adjoining one root also supplies the other.

2. Find the splitting field of \(x^3-2\) over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2},\zeta_3),
   \]
   of degree six.  
   Implication: adjoining a real root need not supply its nonreal conjugates.

3. Find the splitting field of \(x^4-2\) over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \mathbb Q(\sqrt [jmilne](https://www.jmilne.org/math/CourseNotes/FT.pdf){2},i),
   \]
   of degree eight.  
   Implication: root extraction and roots of unity play distinct roles.

4. List the \(\mathbb Q\)-embeddings of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2})\) into \(\mathbb C\).  
   Answer checkpoint: send \(\alpha\) to \(\alpha,\alpha\zeta_3,\alpha\zeta_3^2\).  
   Implication: embeddings detect all conjugates, even those outside the original field.

5. Determine \(\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathbb Q}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2}))\).  
   Answer checkpoint: only the identity.  
   Implication: three embeddings into \(\mathbb C\) do not mean three automorphisms of the original field.

6. Determine all automorphisms of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)/\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: the four independent sign changes.  
   Implication: a map is determined by generator images, but those images must respect algebraic relations.

7. Show that swapping \(\sqrt2\) and \(\sqrt3\) cannot define a \(\mathbb Q\)-automorphism.  
   Answer checkpoint: the relation \((\sqrt2)^2=2\) would be sent to \(3=2\).  
   Implication: not every visually plausible root permutation preserves the field structure.

8. Prove that a \(K\)-embedding sends an algebraic element to a root of its minimal polynomial.  
   Answer checkpoint: apply the embedding to the polynomial relation.  
   Implication: minimal polynomials restrict possible symmetries.

9. Prove that a \(K\)-automorphism of a splitting field is determined by its action on the roots.  
   Answer checkpoint: the roots generate the field.  
   Implication: the Galois group embeds faithfully into a permutation group.

10. Explain why two splitting fields of the same polynomial over \(K\) are \(K\)-isomorphic.  
    Answer checkpoint: extend embeddings by adjoining roots successively.  
    Implication: the splitting field is intrinsic up to isomorphism, even though its concrete presentation is not unique.

## 4. Separability, normality, and Galois extensions

### Motivation

Why do some extensions have as many automorphisms as their degree, while others do not? Two different failures must be separated: repeated conjugates, and conjugates missing from the field. These lead to separability and normality. [pub.math.leidenuniv](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~stevenhagenp/GaloisTheory.pdf)

1. Prove that \(f\) has a repeated root in an algebraic closure exactly when \(\gcd(f,f')\ne1\).  
   Answer checkpoint: compare the factorization of \(f\) with its derivative.  
   Implication: repeated-root questions become polynomial gcd calculations.

2. Prove that every irreducible polynomial over a characteristic-zero field is separable.  
   Answer checkpoint: an irreducible \(f\) cannot divide its nonzero derivative of smaller degree.  
   Implication: over \(\mathbb Q\), separability is automatic; normality remains the main issue.

3. Over \(K=\mathbb F_p(t)\), prove that \(x^p-t\) is irreducible and inseparable.  
   Answer checkpoint: Eisenstein in \(\mathbb F_p[t][x]\) gives irreducibility; the derivative is zero.  
   Implication: positive characteristic introduces phenomena invisible over \(\mathbb Q\).

4. If \(\alpha^p=t\), determine the \(K\)-automorphisms of \(K(\alpha)\).  
   Answer checkpoint: only the identity, despite degree \(p\).  
   Implication: inseparability destroys the expected supply of distinct conjugates.

5. Show that \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2})/\mathbb Q\) is separable but not normal.  
   Answer checkpoint: the other roots of \(x^3-2\) are absent.  
   Implication: separability alone does not make an extension Galois.

6. Show that \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)/\mathbb Q\) is normal and separable.  
   Answer checkpoint: it is the splitting field of \((x^2-2)(x^2-3)\).  
   Implication: splitting fields of separable polynomials give the basic Galois examples.

7. Prove that an irreducible polynomial over a finite field cannot have zero derivative.  
   Answer checkpoint: if its derivative is zero, surjectivity of Frobenius on the coefficients makes it a \(p\)-th power.  
   Implication: finite fields are perfect.

8. Exhibit a normal extension that is not separable.  
   Answer checkpoint: \(\mathbb F_p(t^{1/p})/\mathbb F_p(t)\). Every element has a \(p\)-th power in the base field and only one distinct conjugate.  
   Implication: normality and separability are independent requirements.

9. Prove that a finite separable extension has exactly its degree many embeddings into an algebraic closure.  
   Answer checkpoint: extend embeddings one algebraic generator at a time.  
   Implication: degree counts conjugates when repeated roots do not interfere.

10. Prove that a finite extension \(L/K\) is Galois exactly when
    \[
    |\operatorname{Aut}_K(L)|=[L:K].
    \]
    Answer checkpoint: equality forces all conjugates to be distinct and all embeddings to land in \(L\).  
    Implication: the full degree is visible as internal symmetry precisely in the Galois case.

## 5. The fundamental theorem of Galois theory

### Motivation

Directly searching for every intermediate field is difficult. The fundamental theorem replaces that search with the more manageable task of listing subgroups. Its proof depends on understanding fixed fields and the independence of embeddings. [pub.math.leidenuniv](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~stevenhagenp/GaloisTheory.pdf)

1. Find the entire correspondence for \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)/\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: two subgroups, two fields, no proper intermediate field.  
   Implication: prime degree limits the possible intermediate degrees.

2. Find the entire correspondence for \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)/\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: the three order-two subgroups correspond to \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\), \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)\), and \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)\).  
   Implication: subgroup enumeration proves that this list is complete.

3. Find the entire correspondence for the splitting field of \(x^3-2\).  
   Answer checkpoint: one quadratic field and three cubic fields, corresponding to the four proper nontrivial subgroups of \(S_3\).  
   Implication: intermediate fields need not themselves be Galois.

4. Find all intermediate fields of \(\mathbb F_{p^6}/\mathbb F_p\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(\mathbb F_p,\mathbb F_{p^2},\mathbb F_{p^3},\mathbb F_{p^6}\).  
   Implication: cyclic-group subgroup structure translates into divisibility of field degrees.

5. Find all intermediate fields of \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_5)/\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: the unique proper intermediate field is \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\).  
   Implication: a cyclic group of order four has a unique subgroup of order two.

6. Prove that \(H_1\subseteq H_2\) implies \(L^{H_2}\subseteq L^{H_1}\).  
   Answer checkpoint: being fixed by more automorphisms is a stronger condition.  
   Implication: the lattice reversal is conceptually unavoidable.

7. For intermediate fields \(E_1,E_2\), prove
   \[
   \operatorname{Gal}(L/E_1E_2)
   =\operatorname{Gal}(L/E_1)\cap\operatorname{Gal}(L/E_2).
   \]
   Answer checkpoint: fixing the compositum means fixing both fields.  
   Implication: joining fields corresponds to intersecting subgroups.

8. For subgroups \(H_1,H_2\), prove
   \[
   L^{\langle H_1,H_2\rangle}=L^{H_1}\cap L^{H_2}.
   \]
   Answer checkpoint: an element fixed by both subgroups is fixed by everything they generate.  
   Implication: intersecting fields corresponds to generating a larger subgroup.

9. Prove that \(E/K\) is Galois exactly when \(\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)\) is normal in \(G\).  
   Answer checkpoint: examine restriction maps and conjugation of the subgroup.  
   Implication: normality of a field extension becomes normality of a subgroup.

10. Prove Artin’s fixed-field theorem for a finite group \(H\) of field automorphisms:
    \[
    [L:L^H]=|H|.
    \]
    Answer checkpoint: use linear independence of distinct automorphisms; elementary proofs also use orbit polynomials.  
    Implication: this is a foundational result that makes the correspondence work, not merely a counting trick.

## 6. Computing Galois groups

### Motivation

The correspondence is powerful only when you know the group. The practical task is to extract enough information from irreducibility, discriminants, real roots, and modular factorization to identify it.

1. Compute the Galois group of \(x^2-2\) over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(C_2\).  
   Implication: the first nontrivial root symmetry is a sign change.

2. Compute the Galois group of \(x^3-2\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(S_3\); its discriminant is \(-108\), not a rational square.  
   Implication: an irreducible cubic distinguishes the cyclic and full symmetric cases through its discriminant.

3. Compute the Galois group of \(x^3-3x+1\).  
   Answer checkpoint: it is irreducible and has discriminant \(81\), so the group is \(A_3\cong C_3\).  
   Implication: not every irreducible cubic has group \(S_3\).

4. Compute the Galois group of \((x^2-2)(x^2-3)\).  
   Answer checkpoint: \(C_2\times C_2\).  
   Implication: reducible polynomials can still have informative splitting fields.

5. Compute the Galois group of \(x^4-2\).  
   Answer checkpoint: the dihedral group of order eight, often denoted \(D_4\) or \(D_8\), depending on convention.  
   Implication: quartic root symmetries need not form all of \(S_4\).

6. Compute the Galois group of \(x^4+1\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)/\mathbb Q)
   \cong(\mathbb Z/8\mathbb Z)^\times
   \cong C_2\times C_2.
   \]
   Implication: irreducible quartics can have quite different groups.

7. Prove that the Galois group of an irreducible polynomial acts transitively on its roots.  
   Answer checkpoint: embeddings taking one root to another extend to the splitting field.  
   Implication: irreducibility supplies a strong group-theoretic constraint.

8. Prove that the Galois group of a separable polynomial over a characteristic-not-two field lies in \(A_n\) exactly when its discriminant is a square in the base field.  
   Answer checkpoint: study how permutations act on
   \[
   \prod_{i<j}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j).
   \]
   Implication: the discriminant records permutation parity.

9. Factor \(x^3-2\) modulo \(5\) and interpret the factor degrees.  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   x^3-2=(x-3)(x^2+3x+4)
   \]
   in \(\mathbb F_5[x]\), with the quadratic irreducible. The pattern \(1+2\) corresponds to a transposition.  
   Implication: reduction modulo suitable primes detects cycle types in the Galois group.

10. Prove that \(x^5-2\) has Galois group \(C_5\rtimes C_4\), of order twenty.  
    Answer checkpoint: use the splitting field \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_theory){2},\zeta_5)\), its degree, and the action on roots indexed modulo five.  
    Implication: a quintic can have a much smaller group than \(S_5\).

## 7. Solvable groups, radicals, and quintics

### Motivation

This is the original equation-solving question: what distinguishes an equation whose roots can be written using radicals from one whose roots cannot? In characteristic zero, the decisive criterion is solvability of its Galois group. [people.maths.ox.ac](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/rossler/mypage/pdf-files/GTnotesDR.pdf)

1. Compute the derived series of \(S_3\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   S_3\supset A_3\supset\{1\}.
   \]
   Implication: the group is solvable, consistent with radical formulas for cubics.

2. Compute the derived series of \(S_4\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   S_4\supset A_4\supset V_4\supset\{1\}.
   \]
   Implication: quartic radical formulas reflect solvable group structure.

3. Prove that \(A_5\) is simple and nonabelian.  
   Answer checkpoint: use its conjugacy-class sizes \(1,12,12,15,20\) to rule out proper nontrivial normal subgroups.  
   Implication: a nonabelian simple group cannot be broken into abelian quotient stages.

4. Deduce that \(S_5\) is not solvable.  
   Answer checkpoint: its commutator subgroup is \(A_5\), whose commutator subgroup is itself.  
   Implication: the obstruction to the general quintic formula is structural.

5. Show explicitly that \(x^5-2=0\) is solvable by radicals.  
   Answer checkpoint: its roots are \(\sqrt [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_theory){2}\zeta_5^j\); the required roots of unity also lie in a radical extension.  
   Implication: “quintic” does not mean “unsolvable by radicals.”

6. Prove that \(x^5-4x+2\) is not solvable by radicals over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: Eisenstein at \(2\) gives irreducibility. Analyze the derivative to show exactly three real roots. Complex conjugation is a transposition; a transitive prime-degree group containing a transposition is \(S_5\).  
   Implication: elementary calculus plus group theory can certify radical impossibility. This is a standard representative exercise. [webhomes.maths.ed.ac](https://webhomes.maths.ed.ac.uk/~tl/galois/workshops/2021/ws5_students.pdf)

7. Prove that every subgroup and quotient of a solvable group is solvable.  
   Answer checkpoint: compare their derived series with that of the original group.  
   Implication: solvability behaves well when passing to subextensions and quotient symmetries.

8. If \(K\) contains \(\zeta_n\), \(\operatorname{char}K\nmid n\), and \(\alpha^n\in K\), prove that \(K(\alpha)/K\) is cyclic Galois of degree dividing \(n\).  
   Answer checkpoint: all roots are \(\zeta_n^j\alpha\), and automorphisms embed into the cyclic group of \(n\)-th roots of unity.  
   Implication: roots of unity make radical adjunctions compatible with cyclic symmetry.

9. Explain why adjoining radicals does not always produce a Galois extension.  
   Answer checkpoint: \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2})/\mathbb Q\) is a counterexample.  
   Implication: radical solvability must be studied through a suitable larger Galois extension.

10. Outline both directions of the radical-solvability theorem.  
    Answer checkpoint: radical towers lead to solvable groups after adjoining roots of unity and taking suitable closures; solvable groups can be refined into cyclic prime-order stages, then realized through radical constructions.  
    Implication: the theorem is a bridge between formulas and symmetry, not simply an assertion that groups resemble radicals. [people.maths.ox.ac](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/rossler/mypage/pdf-files/GTnotesDR.pdf)

## 8. Finite fields and cyclotomic fields

### Motivation

These two families make Galois theory explicit. Finite fields expose Frobenius symmetry; cyclotomic fields expose the arithmetic of roots of unity. Both are central destinations of a first course. [courses.maths.ox.ac](https://courses.maths.ox.ac.uk/course/section.php?id=10838)

1. Construct \(\mathbb F_4\) as a quotient of \(\mathbb F_2[x]\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[x]/(x^2+x+1).
   \]
   Implication: irreducible-polynomial quotients build concrete fields.

2. Show that the nonzero elements of \(\mathbb F_4\) form a cyclic group of order three.  
   Answer checkpoint: if \(\alpha^2+\alpha+1=0\), then \(\alpha^3=1\), with \(\alpha\ne1\).  
   Implication: additive and multiplicative structures of a field are very different.

3. Construct \(\mathbb F_9\), and find an element of multiplicative order eight.  
   Answer checkpoint: use \(\mathbb F_3[i]\) with \(i^2=-1\); \(1+i\) has order eight.  
   Implication: a finite field’s multiplicative group can be cyclic even when its additive group is not.

4. Prove that a finite field has \(p^n\) elements for some prime \(p\).  
   Answer checkpoint: view it as a finite-dimensional vector space over its prime field \(\mathbb F_p\).  
   Implication: field cardinality is controlled by characteristic and dimension.

5. Prove that \(\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb F_{p^n}/\mathbb F_p)\) is generated by \(x\mapsto x^p\).  
   Answer checkpoint: Frobenius has order \(n\), giving the required \(n\) automorphisms.  
   Implication: one explicit map accounts for every symmetry.

6. Determine the subfields of \(\mathbb F_{p^{12}}\).  
   Answer checkpoint: exactly \(\mathbb F_{p^d}\) for \(d=1,2,3,4,6,12\).  
   Implication: intermediate-field structure becomes divisor arithmetic. [webhomes.maths.ed.ac](https://webhomes.maths.ed.ac.uk/~tl/galois/workshops/2021/ws5_students.pdf)

7. Prove that \(\Phi_p(x)=1+x+\cdots+x^{p-1}\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) for prime \(p\).  
   Answer checkpoint: apply Eisenstein at \(p\) to \(\Phi_p(x+1)\).  
   Implication: primitive roots of unity have predictable degrees.

8. Compute the Galois group of \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_7)/\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   (\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z)^\times\cong C_6.
   \]
   Implication: automorphisms are encoded by exponent arithmetic.

9. Identify the maximal real subfield of \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_7)\) and its degree.  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \mathbb Q(\zeta_7+\zeta_7^{-1}),
   \qquad [\,\cdot:\mathbb Q]=3.
   \]
   Implication: complex conjugation gives a natural fixed field.

10. Find all quadratic subfields of \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)\).  
    Answer checkpoint:
    \[
    \mathbb Q(i),\qquad \mathbb Q(\sqrt2),\qquad \mathbb Q(\sqrt{-2}).
    \]
    Implication: cyclotomic fields connect roots of unity with familiar radical fields.

## 9. Supporting theorems and classical applications

### Motivation

The theory also answers questions that do not initially look like polynomial-solving problems: Can a geometric construction be performed? Can two generators be replaced by one? Can we extract base-field information from an extension? Trace and norm formalize the last question. [math.purdue](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~twooley/2024gt/2024GaloisTheory.pdf)

1. Prove the primitive element theorem for finite separable extensions over an infinite field.  
   Answer checkpoint: for \(K(\alpha,\beta)\), choose \(\alpha+c\beta\) while avoiding finitely many bad values of \(c\in K\).  
   Implication: separability permits a finite extension to be encoded by one element.

2. Handle the finite-base-field case of the primitive element theorem.  
   Answer checkpoint: a generator of the finite extension’s multiplicative group generates the field.  
   Implication: a different argument handles the case where “avoid finitely many values” is unavailable.

3. Find the normal closure of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2})/\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \mathbb Q(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2},\zeta_3).
   \]
   Implication: the normal closure adds the missing conjugates, providing the correct field for symmetry analysis.

4. Prove that the field of algebraic numbers in \(\mathbb C\) is algebraically closed.  
   Answer checkpoint: roots of polynomials with algebraic coefficients are algebraic over a finite extension of \(\mathbb Q\), hence over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Implication: algebraic closures organize all algebraic roots into one ambient field.

5. Prove that an algebraic straightedge-and-compass constructible real number has degree a power of two over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: constructions produce a tower of extensions of degree at most two.  
   Implication: a geometric procedure imposes an algebraic degree restriction.

6. Prove that duplicating the cube is impossible with straightedge and compass.  
   Answer checkpoint: \(\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2}\) has degree three.  
   Implication: impossibility follows from degree arithmetic, not failure to find a clever drawing.

7. Prove that an angle of \(60^\circ\) cannot be trisected with straightedge and compass.  
   Answer checkpoint: \(u=2\cos20^\circ\) satisfies the irreducible cubic
   \[
   u^3-3u-1=0.
   \]
   Implication: trigonometry converts a construction problem into an extension-degree obstruction.

8. Show that a regular pentagon is constructible.  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \cos72^\circ=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{4}.
   \]
   Implication: cyclotomic symmetries can establish possibility as well as impossibility.

9. Compute trace and norm in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt d)/\mathbb Q\), for squarefree \(d\ne1\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \operatorname{Tr}(a+b\sqrt d)=2a,\qquad
   N(a+b\sqrt d)=a^2-db^2.
   \]
   Implication: adding and multiplying conjugates produces quantities in the base field. [math.stanford](http://math.stanford.edu/~conrad/154Page/handouts/normtrace.pdf)

10. Compute the trace and norm of \(\alpha=\sqrt [math.dartmouth](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~auel/courses/370s19/docs/syllabus.pdf){2}\) over \(\mathbb Q\).  
    Answer checkpoint: trace \(0\), norm \(2\); recover these from the multiplication matrix or the minimal polynomial.  
    Implication: trace and norm remain meaningful even when the extension is not Galois. [math.stanford](http://math.stanford.edu/~conrad/154Page/handouts/normtrace.pdf)

## 10. Advanced directions

### Motivation

After finite Galois theory, the question broadens: How do infinitely many extensions fit together? Which groups can occur? How do Galois groups act on geometric objects? These are several distinct branches, not a single linear continuation. The inverse Galois problem, for example, asks whether every finite group occurs over \(\mathbb Q\). [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.13719v4)

The problems below are entry points; several are theorem-sized projects.

1. Compute
   \[
   \operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb F}_p/\mathbb F_p).
   \]
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \widehat{\mathbb Z},
   \]
   with Frobenius as a topological generator.  
   Implication: infinite Galois groups require topology, not only abstract group structure.

2. Describe \(\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_{2^\infty})/\mathbb Q)\), where the field is the union of all \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_{2^n})\).  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   \varprojlim_n(\mathbb Z/2^n\mathbb Z)^\times
   =\mathbb Z_2^\times.
   \]
   Implication: compatible finite automorphisms assemble into a profinite group.

3. Explain why the infinite Galois correspondence uses closed subgroups.  
   Answer checkpoint: a subgroup and its closure have the same fixed field; Frobenius generates a proper dense subgroup of \(\widehat{\mathbb Z}\).  
   Implication: without the closedness condition, the correspondence is not injective.

4. Realize \(C_2\), \(C_3\), \(V_4\), and \(S_3\) as Galois groups over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: use earlier quadratic, cyclic cubic, biquadratic, and cubic splitting-field examples.  
   Implication: this reverses the ordinary question—start with a group and seek an equation.

5. Prove that every finite abelian group occurs as a Galois group over \(\mathbb Q\).  
   Answer checkpoint: realize it as a quotient of a suitable cyclotomic Galois group; choosing suitable distinct primes uses Dirichlet’s theorem.  
   Implication: cyclotomic theory solves a substantial part of the inverse Galois problem.

6. Show that \(x^3-2\) has modular factorization types \(3\), \(1+2\), and \(1+1+1\) at the primes \(7\), \(5\), and \(31\), respectively.  
   Answer checkpoint: these correspond to three-cycles, transpositions, and the identity.  
   Implication: prime splitting reveals the same group through arithmetic rather than explicit root formulas.

7. For the elliptic curve \(E:y^2=x^3-x\), determine its two-torsion and the Galois action on it.  
   Answer checkpoint:
   \[
   E [people.math.carleton](https://people.math.carleton.ca/~mezo/galoistop.pdf)=\{O,(0,0),(1,0),(-1,0)\},
   \]
   all rational, so the action is trivial.  
   Implication: Galois groups can act on geometric objects’ finite algebraic data.

8. For a finite Galois extension \(L/K\), prove the normal basis theorem; then find a normal basis of \(\mathbb F_4/\mathbb F_2\).  
   Answer checkpoint: for \(\alpha^2+\alpha+1=0\), the pair \(\alpha,\alpha^2\) is a basis.  
   Implication: one element’s Galois orbit can encode the entire vector-space structure. [math.rwth-aachen](https://www.math.rwth-aachen.de/~Michael.Mertens/GaloisTheory.pdf)

9. Prove cyclic Hilbert theorem 90:
   \[
   N_{L/K}(a)=1
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   a=\frac{b}{\sigma(b)}
   \]
   when \(L/K\) is cyclic with generator \(\sigma\).  
   Answer checkpoint: construct \(b\) using a weighted sum of conjugates and use independence of automorphisms to ensure a nonzero choice.  
   Implication: a norm condition becomes an explicit multiplicative equation; this is a gateway to Galois cohomology.

10. Show that
    \[
    \mathbb F_p(s^{1/p},t^{1/p})/\mathbb F_p(s,t)
    \]
    has degree \(p^2\) but is not simple.  
    Answer checkpoint: every element has its \(p\)-th power in the base field, so every simple subextension has degree at most \(p\).  
    Implication: the separability hypothesis in the primitive element theorem is essential.

## How the problems motivate the theory

The conceptual progression is:

| Problem you encounter | Tool you are led to develop |
|---|---|
| “How much new arithmetic does this root create?” | Minimal polynomials, bases, extension degrees |
| “Why does adjoining one root miss the others?” | Splitting fields and normal closures |
| “Which substitutions of roots are legitimate?” | Embeddings and automorphisms |
| “Why are there fewer symmetries than the degree suggests?” | Separability and normality |
| “How can I find every intermediate field?” | Fixed fields and the fundamental theorem |
| “What is the actual symmetry group of this equation?” | Discriminants, cycle types, modular factorization |
| “Why do radical formulas succeed or fail?” | Solvable groups and radical towers |
| “Can this geometric construction be done?” | Quadratic towers and constructibility |
| “How do all these extensions fit together?” | Profinite groups and infinite Galois theory |

For your interest in quintics, the most direct first pass is Sections 1–7, interleaving the cyclotomic problems from Section 8 before the proof of radical solvability. That order matches the central progression of standard courses toward the insolvability of the general quintic. [courses.maths.ox.ac](https://courses.maths.ox.ac.uk/course/section.php?id=10838)

For each computational problem, record four items: the field, its degree, its automorphisms, and what those automorphisms tell you. That makes the examples a connected investigation rather than a collection of isolated calculations.
