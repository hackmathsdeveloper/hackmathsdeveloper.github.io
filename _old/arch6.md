
Archimedes’ *On the Equilibrium of Planes*, also known as *On the Centres of Gravity of Planes*, contains **25 propositions** in two books: 15 in Book I and 10 in Book II. Book I develops the law of the lever and the centres of gravity of rectilinear figures; Book II determines centres of gravity of parabolic segments and portions cut off between parallel chords. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Equilibrium_of_Planes)

Below is a proposition-by-proposition catalogue, including the explicit corollaries to I.5. The statements are paraphrased; formulas translate the geometrical results into modern notation. “Centre of gravity” is understood here as the centroid of a uniform plane lamina.

## Book I — Equilibrium and centres of gravity of polygons

### I.1 — Weights balancing at equal distances are equal

If two weights balance at equal distances from the fulcrum, the weights must be equal:

\[
d_1=d_2,\quad \text{equilibrium}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad W_1=W_2.
\]

This is the converse of the opening assumption that equal weights balance at equal distances. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Intro.I.Props1-5/Intro.I.Props1-5.html)

### I.2 — At equal distances, the heavier weight prevails

Unequal weights placed at equal distances do not balance: the system inclines toward the heavier weight. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Intro.I.Props1-5/Intro.I.Props1-5.html)

### I.3 — A heavier weight balances a lighter one only at a shorter distance

If unequal weights balance, their distances are unequal, with the heavier weight nearer the fulcrum.

Conversely, if weights balance at unequal distances, the weight at the shorter distance is heavier. This establishes the direction of the relationship before its exact ratio is proved. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Intro.I.Props1-5/Intro.I.Props1-5.html)

### I.4 — Centre of gravity of two equal magnitudes

The centre of gravity of two equal magnitudes lies at the midpoint of the straight segment joining their individual centres of gravity. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Intro.I.Props1-5/Intro.I.Props1-5.html)

### I.5 — Centre of gravity of three equally spaced equal magnitudes

If three equal magnitudes have collinear, equally spaced centres of gravity, their combined centre of gravity is the centre of the middle magnitude. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Intro.I.Props1-5/Intro.I.Props1-5.html)

The proposition has two explicit generalizations:

- Odd number of magnitudes: if the centres are equally spaced on a line and magnitudes equally far from the middle are equal, the combined centre is the centre of the middle magnitude.
- Even number of magnitudes: under the corresponding paired-equality and equal-spacing conditions, the combined centre is the midpoint between the two middle centres. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Intro.I.Props1-5/Intro.I.Props1-5.html)

### I.6 — Law of the lever for commensurable magnitudes

Commensurable magnitudes—those having a common measure—balance at distances inversely proportional to their weights:

\[
\frac{d_1}{d_2}=\frac{W_2}{W_1},
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
W_1d_1=W_2d_2.
\]

This is the commensurable case of the law of the lever. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Equilibrium_of_Planes)

### I.7 — Law of the lever for incommensurable magnitudes

The same inverse-proportion rule holds when the magnitudes are incommensurable, extending the result beyond ratios expressible as ratios of whole numbers. Together, I.6–I.7 establish the general lever law in the treatise. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Equilibrium_of_Planes)

### I.8 — Centre of gravity after removing a part

If a part is removed from a magnitude and its centre differs from that of the whole, the centre of the remainder lies on the line joining those two centres, extended beyond the centre of the whole, away from the removed part.

If the whole has weight \(W\) and centre \(G\), and the removed part has weight \(w\) and centre \(P\), then the remainder’s centre \(R\) satisfies

\[
\frac{GR}{GP}=\frac{w}{W-w}.
\]

In modern vector notation,

\[
\mathbf r_R
=
\frac{W\mathbf r_G-w\mathbf r_P}{W-w}.
\]

This subtraction principle is central to the later centroid constructions. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.I.8/Prop.I.8.html)

### I.9 — A parallelogram’s centre lies on either line joining opposite-side midpoints

The centre of gravity of a parallelogram lies on the straight line joining the midpoints of either pair of opposite sides. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.I.9-10/Prop.I.9-10.html)

### I.10 — Centre of gravity of a parallelogram

The centre of gravity of a parallelogram is the intersection of its diagonals. The text also supplies an alternative proof of this proposition. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.I.9-10/Prop.I.9-10.html)

### I.11 — Corresponding centres in similar triangles

If two triangles are similar and two points occupy corresponding positions in them, then, if one point is the centre of gravity of its triangle, the other is the centre of gravity of the other triangle. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.I.11-12/Prop.I.11-12.html)

### I.12 — Transfer of the median property between similar triangles

If the centre of gravity of one triangle lies on a median, then the centre of gravity of a similar triangle lies on the corresponding median. This is a preparatory result for establishing the median property for every triangle. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.I.11-12/Prop.I.11-12.html)

### I.13 — A triangle’s centre of gravity lies on each median

In any triangle, the centre of gravity lies on the straight line joining a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. The statement applies to any of its three vertices. [piratesandrevolutionaries.blogspot](https://piratesandrevolutionaries.blogspot.com/2014/04/archimedes-on-equilibrium-of-planes_3773.html)

### I.14 — Centre of gravity of a triangle

The centre of gravity of a triangle is the common intersection of its three medians. The associated consequence used subsequently is that the centre divides each median in the ratio

\[
VG:GM=2:1,
\]

where \(V\) is the vertex and \(M\) the opposite side’s midpoint. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.I.13-14/Prop.I.13-14.html)

### I.15 — Centre of gravity of a trapezium

For a quadrilateral with two parallel sides, the centre lies on the segment joining the midpoints of those sides.

Let the parallel sides have lengths \(B>b\), with midpoints \(M_B,M_b\). Then

\[
\frac{M_bG}{GM_B}
=
\frac{2B+b}{B+2b}.
\]

Equivalently, if their perpendicular separation is \(h\), the centroid’s perpendicular distance from the larger base is

\[
\bar y=\frac{h(B+2b)}{3(B+b)}.
\]

Thus the centroid lies closer to the larger parallel side. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.I.15/Prop.I.15.html)

## Book II — Centres of gravity of parabolic regions

A “parabolic segment” means the plane region bounded by a parabola and a chord. Its diameter joins the chord’s midpoint to the point on the parabolic arc where the tangent is parallel to the chord. This diameter need not be the parabola’s axis, nor need it be perpendicular to the chord. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.2/Prop.II.2.html)

Archimedes also uses a particular sequence of inscribed polygons: begin with a triangle having the segment’s base and height, then add analogous triangles in each remaining parabolic segment, and repeat. The translation cited here calls these “familiarly inscribed” figures. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.2/Prop.II.2.html)

### II.1 — Combined centre of gravity of two parabolic segments

For two parabolic segments whose areas can be applied to a given straight line, the centre of gravity of their combination lies on the segment joining their individual centres, dividing it inversely as their areas.

For areas \(A_1,A_2\), with centres \(G_1,G_2\), the combined centre \(G\) satisfies

\[
\frac{G_1G}{GG_2}=\frac{A_2}{A_1}.
\]

The proposition establishes this specifically for parabolic segments by replacing them with equal-area rectangles. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.1/Prop.II.1.html)

### II.2 — Centre of gravity of an inscribed approximating polygon

The centre of gravity of every polygon inscribed by the prescribed repeated-triangle construction lies on the diameter of the parabolic segment. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.2/Prop.II.2.html)

The introductory geometrical observations also establish that:

- The relevant chords joining paired polygon vertices are parallel to the original base and bisected by the diameter.
- At successive construction levels, the resulting subdivisions of the diameter follow successive odd-number ratios, such as \(1:3:5:7\). [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.2/Prop.II.2.html)

### II.3 — Corresponding polygonal approximations have corresponding centres

For two similar parabolic segments, construct the prescribed inscribed polygons with the same number of sides. Their centres of gravity divide the respective diameters in the same ratio. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.3/Prop.II.3.html)

### II.4 — A parabolic segment’s centre lies on its diameter

The centre of gravity of every parabolic segment lies on its diameter. This extends the result for inscribed polygons to the entire curved region. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.4/Prop.II.4.html)

### II.5 — The segment’s centre is nearer the vertex than an approximating polygon’s centre

The centre of gravity of the complete parabolic segment lies nearer its vertex than the centre of gravity of any polygon inscribed by the prescribed construction. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.5/Prop.II.5.html)

### II.6 — Polygonal centres approach the segment’s centre arbitrarily closely

Given any positive length, however small, one can construct a prescribed inscribed polygon whose centre of gravity is closer than that length to the centre of gravity of the parabolic segment:

\[
\text{for every }\varepsilon>0,
\quad
\text{an inscribed polygon exists with }|G_{\mathrm{polygon}}G|<\varepsilon.
\]

This is a rigorous approximation result for centres of gravity, not merely for areas. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.6/Prop.II.6.html)

### II.7 — Similar parabolic segments have similarly positioned centres

The centres of gravity of similar parabolic segments divide their diameters in the same ratio. The proof uses the corresponding polygonal approximations and the arbitrarily close approximation established in II.6. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.7/Prop.II.7.html)

### II.8 — Exact centre of gravity of a parabolic segment

Let \(V\) be the segment’s vertex, \(M\) its chord’s midpoint, and \(G\) its centre of gravity. Then

\[
VG:GM=3:2.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
VG=\frac35VM,
\qquad
MG=\frac25VM.
\]

Thus the centroid lies three-fifths of the way from the vertex to the base, measured along the diameter. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.8/Prop.II.8.html)

### II.9 — A proportionality lemma for the final centroid construction

This proposition is an algebraic-geometrical lemma, not a separate centroid theorem.

Let four decreasing positive lengths be in continued proportion:

\[
a:b=b:c=c:d.
\]

Define lengths \(x,y\) by

\[
\frac{x}{\frac35(a-c)}=\frac{d}{a-d}
\]

and

\[
\frac{y}{a-c}
=
\frac{2a+4b+6c+3d}
{5a+10b+10c+5d}.
\]

Then

\[
x+y=\frac25a.
\]

This modern symbolic statement reproduces the proportionality result used in II.10. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.9/Prop.II.9.html)

### II.10 — Centre of gravity of a parabolic portion between parallel chords

Consider the region bounded by two parallel chords and the two intervening arcs of the parabola. Its centre of gravity lies on the diameter joining the chords’ midpoints.

Let:

- \(M_s\) be the midpoint of the smaller chord;
- \(M_L\) be the midpoint of the larger chord;
- \(H=M_sM_L\);
- \(s,L\) be the smaller and larger chord lengths;
- \(P,Q\) be points on the diameter with
  \[
  M_sP=\frac25H,\qquad M_sQ=\frac35H.
  \]

Then the centre \(G\) lies within the middle fifth \(PQ\) and satisfies

\[
\frac{PG}{GQ}
=
\frac{L^2(2s+L)}{s^2(2L+s)}.
\]

Archimedes states this ratio using volumes of solids with square bases and specified heights; the expression above is its algebraic equivalent. Using half-chord lengths instead of full chord lengths gives the same ratio. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.II.10/Prop.II.10.html)

## Assumptions, distinguished from derived results

Book I begins with seven postulates. These are not additional proved propositions, but they specify the framework in which the 25 results are derived:

1. Equal weights balance at equal distances; at unequal distances, the farther equal weight prevails.
2. Adding weight to one side of an equilibrium makes that side prevail.
3. Removing weight from one side makes the other side prevail.
4. Congruent plane figures have coincident centres of gravity when superposed.
5. Similar figures have similarly situated centres of gravity.
6. Equal magnitudes can replace balancing magnitudes at the same distances without disturbing equilibrium.
7. The centre of gravity of a convex figure lies inside it. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Intro.I.Props1-5/Intro.I.Props1-5.html)

The work therefore progresses from balance principles, through rules for combining and subtracting magnitudes, to polygonal centroids, and finally to curved regions by geometrical exhaustion. Its culminating curved-region results are II.8, for a complete parabolic segment, and II.10, for a portion between parallel chords. [web.calstatela](https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/Archimedes/De%20Planorum%20%20Aeqilibriis/Prop.I.8/Prop.I.8.html)
