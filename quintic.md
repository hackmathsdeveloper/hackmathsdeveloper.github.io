A quintic equation always has five complex roots. What **does not exist** is a single general formula for all quintics using only arithmetic operations and finitely many radicals (square roots, cube roots, etc.). The group-theoretic reason is that a generic quintic has Galois group \(S_5\), which is not a solvable group. [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/QuinticEquation.html)

## Roots versus formulas

For
\[
f(x)=x^5+a_4x^4+a_3x^3+a_2x^2+a_1x+a_0,
\]
the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees five roots in \(\mathbb C\), counted with multiplicity.

The question Abel and Galois settled is instead:

> Can every such root be expressed from the coefficients using \(+,-,\times,\div\), and repeated \(n\)-th roots?

For degrees 2, 3, and 4, yes. For degree 5 in general, no. Some individual quintics are nevertheless solvable by radicals. [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/QuinticEquation.html)

## The Galois-group connection

Let \(K\) be the field generated by the coefficients, often \(K=\mathbb Q\). Put all roots of \(f\) into a splitting field \(L\).

The **Galois group**
\[
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
\]
consists of field automorphisms of \(L\) that leave the coefficients fixed. Each automorphism permutes the roots, so it is a subgroup of \(S_5\).

A central theorem of Galois theory says, in characteristic zero:

\[
\boxed{\text{\(f\) is solvable by radicals} \iff
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\text{ is a solvable group}.}
\]

Radical expressions impose a structural constraint: their Galois groups can be dismantled in layers whose quotients are abelian. That property is exactly group-theoretic solvability.

## Why \(S_5\) blocks radicals

A generic degree-5 polynomial has no special symmetry or algebraic relation among its roots, so every permutation of five roots can occur. Its Galois group is therefore

\[
S_5.
\]

Now inspect its commutator/derived series:

\[
S_5^{(0)} = S_5,
\qquad
S_5^{(1)}=[S_5,S_5]=A_5,
\qquad
S_5^{(2)}=[A_5,A_5]=A_5.
\]

The crucial obstruction is \(A_5\):

- \(A_5\) is **non-abelian**.
- \(A_5\) is **simple**: it has no nontrivial proper normal subgroups.
- It is **perfect**: its commutator subgroup is itself, \( [A_5,A_5]=A_5 \).

Thus, once the derived series reaches \(A_5\), it never shrinks to the identity:

\[
S_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset \cdots
\]

So \(S_5\) is not solvable. Consequently, a quintic whose Galois group is \(S_5\) cannot have its roots expressed by radicals.

## Important qualification

It is wrong to say “quintic roots do not exist” or “no quintic can be solved.” The correct statement is:

\[
\boxed{\text{There is no radicals-only formula that solves every quintic.}}
\]

For example, \(x^5-2=0\) is trivially solvable:
\[
x=\sqrt [youtube](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XYgS5VOXvUs){2}\,\zeta_5^k,\qquad k=0,\dots,4.
\]

Its Galois group is solvable, unlike \(S_5\). A quintic is solvable by radicals precisely when its particular Galois group is solvable—not merely because it has degree five. [mathworld.wolfram](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/QuinticEquation.html)
