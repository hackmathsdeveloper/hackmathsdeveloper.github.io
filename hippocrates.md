
Hippocrates of Chios (c. 470–410 BCE) was a Greek mathematician and astronomer, not to be confused with Hippocrates of Cos (the physician). His mathematical results are known mainly through later commentators, especially Simplicius and Proclus, since none of his own works survive. Here are the principal mathematical results attributed to him:

## 1. The Lunes of Hippocrates (Hippocrates' Lunes)
His most famous result: he showed that certain crescent-shaped regions (lunes) bounded by circular arcs have areas equal to certain rectilinear figures. Specifically:

- **Lune on the side of a right isosceles triangle:** If a right isosceles triangle is inscribed in a semicircle, and a semicircle is drawn on one of the legs, the lune between them equals the area of the triangle (or half the triangle, depending on the version). This was the first known **quadrature of a curvilinear figure** — showing a region bounded by curves equal in area to a rectilinear figure.
- **Lune on the hypotenuse variant:** Related configurations where lunes on the legs of a right triangle are squared.
- **Generalization to a trapezoid:** A lune constructed on the side of an isosceles trapezoid inscribed in a circle can be squared.

These results are reported by Simplicius (drawing on Eudemus's history of geometry) and are the earliest documented attempts at **squaring the circle** by means of lunes.

## 2. Quadrature of lunes as a step toward circle-squaring
Hippocrates used the lune results in an attempted quadrature of the circle — though the attempt was fallacious (the fallacy being the assumption that a lune could be squared for any arc, or an unjustified generalization). This is historically important as the first recorded "reduction" of a circle-squaring problem to another curvilinear quadrature.

## 3. The "Hippocrates' theorem" on similar segments (attributed context)
In the lune arguments, the key lemma used is that **similar segments of circles are to one another as the squares on their bases** (Euclid XII.2 later formalizes the analogous result for circles). Hippocrates is credited with using this circle-segment proportionality.

## 4. Elements of geometry (lost)
Hippocrates is credited by later sources (e.g., Proclus, and the scholia) with compiling an early **"Elements" (Stoicheia)** — a systematic textbook of geometry preceding Euclid's. Its exact contents are unknown, but it reportedly included the lune quadratures and early work on the theory of proportions and possibly on irrationals. Some scholars associate him with early treatment of the **theory of means/proportions** and with the problem of **doubling the cube** (the Delian problem), though the cube-duplication attribution is debated and often assigned to Hippocrates of Chios in connection with reducing it to finding two mean proportionals.

## 5. Reduction of the duplication of the cube to two mean proportionals
Hippocrates is credited (via Eutocius/Proclus traditions) with reducing the problem of **doubling the cube** to the problem of finding **two mean proportionals** between a and 2a. This is a genuinely important mathematical insight, though the historical attribution is not entirely secure.

## Summary list
- Quadrature of the lune on the side of a right isosceles triangle (and variants).
- Quadrature of a lune on the side of an isosceles trapezoid.
- Use of the theorem that similar circular segments are as the squares on their bases.
- First quadrature of a curvilinear figure (lune) — a landmark in Greek geometry.
- Attempted (fallacious) quadrature of the circle via lunes.
- Early "Elements" of geometry (lost; contents uncertain).
- Reduction of cube-duplication to finding two mean proportionals (attribution debated).

**Caveat:** Because none of Hippocrates' writings survive, all these are reconstructions from Simplicius, Proclus, Eutocius, and later doxographical sources; the precise statements and attributions carry some uncertainty.
