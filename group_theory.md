

There is no finite, universally agreed list of “all types of groups.” Instead, groups are classified along several **overlapping** axes: their size, commutativity, generators, normal-subgroup structure, representations, and any additional topology or geometry. A single group can belong to many classes simultaneously. Group theory distinguishes these intrinsic properties from concrete realizations as permutations, matrices, or transformations. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

Below is a systematic map of the main classes, followed by what mathematicians mean by “classifying” them.

## 1. Starting point: what is a group?

A group is a set \(G\) with a binary operation \(G\times G\to G\), usually written \(ab\), satisfying:

1. Associativity: \((ab)c=a(bc)\).
2. Identity: there is an \(e\in G\) with \(ea=ae=a\).
3. Inverses: every \(a\in G\) has an \(a^{-1}\) with \(aa^{-1}=a^{-1}a=e\).

Closure is already included in requiring the operation to map \(G\times G\) into \(G\). Commutativity is not required. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics))

For example, \((\mathbb Z,+)\) is a group, with identity \(0\) and inverse \(-a\). The operation matters: one must distinguish a set from a group structure on that set. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics))

## 2. Classification by size, generators, and element orders

These describe three different kinds of finiteness.

| Type | Defining condition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Trivial group | Exactly one element | \(\{e\}\) |
| Finite group | \(|G|<\infty\); \(|G|\) is its order | \(S_3\), of order \(6\) |
| Infinite group | Infinitely many elements | \((\mathbb Z,+)\) |
| Countable group | Its elements form a finite or countably infinite set | \(\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q\) under addition |
| Uncountable group | Its underlying set is uncountable | \((\mathbb R,+)\) |
| Cyclic group | Generated by one element: \(G=\langle g\rangle\) | \(\mathbb Z\), \(C_n=\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) |
| Finitely generated group | Generated by finitely many elements | \(\mathbb Z^2\), \(S_3\) |
| Finitely presented group | Has finitely many generators and defining relations | \(\mathbb Z^2=\langle a,b\mid ab=ba\rangle\) |
| Free group | Has generators with no relations beyond those forced by the group axioms | \(F_2=\langle a,b\mid\ \rangle\) |

Generators and relations provide a central way to specify abstract groups; finite generation and finite presentation are especially important in combinatorial and geometric group theory. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

Further classifications concern the orders of individual elements:

- Torsion or periodic group: every element has finite order.
- Torsion-free group: only the identity has finite order.
- Mixed group: has both nonidentity finite-order elements and infinite-order elements.
- Group of bounded exponent: there is a fixed positive integer \(n\) such that \(g^n=e\) for every \(g\in G\).
- Finite \(p\)-group: has order \(p^k\), where \(p\) is prime.
- Locally finite group: every finitely generated subgroup is finite.

These are structural properties rather than mutually exclusive named families; periodic groups are one of the standard intrinsic classes studied in abstract group theory. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

For instance, \(\mathbb Z\) is infinite, countable, cyclic, finitely presented, and torsion-free. By contrast, \(C_n\) is finite, cyclic, and torsion.

## 3. Classification by commutativity and internal structure

### Abelian and nonabelian groups

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Abelian group | \(ab=ba\) for all \(a,b\in G\) | \(\mathbb Z^2\), \(C_n\) |
| Nonabelian group | Some \(a,b\) satisfy \(ab\ne ba\) | \(S_3\), square symmetries |

Every cyclic group is abelian, but not every abelian group is cyclic. For example, \(C_2\times C_2\) is abelian but cannot be generated by one element. Commutativity is an additional property, not a group axiom. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics))

### Nilpotent, solvable, and related groups

These classes measure how far a group is from being abelian.

| Type | Defining idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nilpotent group | Its upper central series reaches the whole group after finitely many steps | Every finite \(p\)-group |
| Solvable group | Its derived series reaches the trivial group | \(S_3\) |
| Metabelian group | Its commutator subgroup is abelian | \(S_3\) |
| Perfect group | Equals its own commutator subgroup | \(A_5\) |

Nilpotent and solvable groups are major subjects of finite group theory, while solvability also controls whether polynomial equations can be solved by radicals through their Galois groups. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

To make the distinction precise, let

\[
[G,G]=\langle aba^{-1}b^{-1}:a,b\in G\rangle.
\]

The derived series is

\[
G^{(0)}=G,\qquad G^{(k+1)}=[G^{(k)},G^{(k)}].
\]

A group is solvable when some \(G^{(k)}=\{e\}\). A perfect group satisfies \([G,G]=G\), so a nontrivial perfect group cannot be solvable.

The important chain of implications is

\[
\text{cyclic}\Longrightarrow
\text{abelian}\Longrightarrow
\text{nilpotent}\Longrightarrow
\text{solvable}.
\]

The converses fail. These classes organize intrinsic group structure, independently of whether the elements are represented as permutations or matrices. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

### Simple groups

A simple group is a nontrivial group whose only normal subgroups are \(\{e\}\) and \(G\). It can still have many proper subgroups; they just cannot be normal. Examples include \(C_p\) for prime \(p\), and \(A_n\) for \(n\ge5\). [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

“Simple” therefore does not mean small, easy, or abelian.

## 4. Classification by concrete realization or named family

These describe how a group is represented, constructed, or encountered.

### Permutation and finite symmetry groups

| Family | Description |
|---|---|
| Permutation groups | Subgroups of the bijections of a set, under composition |
| Symmetric groups \(S_n\) | All permutations of \(n\) objects |
| Alternating groups \(A_n\) | All even permutations of \(n\) objects |
| Dihedral groups | Rotations and reflections of a regular polygon |
| Quaternion and generalized quaternion groups | Families extending the quaternion group \(Q_8\) |
| Coxeter groups | Groups presented by involutions with specified relations between pairs |
| Weyl groups | Reflection groups associated with root systems |

Permutation groups and transformation groups are foundational concrete realizations; Weyl groups connect finite reflection symmetry with Lie theory. Dihedral, quaternion, and related families also appear in finite-group catalogues. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

Here, use \(D_{2n}\) for the dihedral group of order \(2n\):

\[
D_{2n}=
\langle r,s\mid r^n=e,\ s^2=e,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle.
\]

Some authors instead call this \(D_n\), so the notation needs checking. The square symmetry group has order \(8\). [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics))

A crucial point: by Cayley’s theorem, every abstract group is isomorphic to a permutation group. Thus “permutation group” is a realization, not a competing alternative to “abelian” or “solvable.” [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

### Matrix groups

A linear or matrix group is a subgroup of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(F)\), the invertible \(n\times n\) matrices over a field \(F\). [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

Important families include:

| Family | Matrices or transformations preserved |
|---|---|
| General linear \(\mathrm{GL}_n(F)\) | All invertible matrices |
| Special linear \(\mathrm{SL}_n(F)\) | Determinant \(1\) |
| Orthogonal \(\mathrm O_n(F)\) | A specified nondegenerate symmetric form |
| Special orthogonal \(\mathrm{SO}_n(F)\) | Orthogonal transformations of determinant \(1\) |
| Unitary \(\mathrm U(n)\) | The standard complex Hermitian inner product |
| Special unitary \(\mathrm{SU}(n)\) | Unitary matrices of determinant \(1\) |
| Symplectic \(\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(F)\) | A specified nondegenerate alternating form |

The underlying field matters: groups over finite fields enter finite group theory, while real and complex matrix groups provide major examples in Lie theory. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

### Groups named by their origin

- Automorphism groups: structure-preserving bijections of a mathematical object.
- Galois groups: field automorphisms fixing a base field.
- Fundamental groups: based loops modulo homotopy, with concatenation.
- Symmetry or isometry groups: transformations preserving a geometric structure or distances.
- Point groups and space groups: groups describing molecular or crystal symmetry.
- Braid groups: groups arising from composition of braids. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

These labels describe origins or actions, not necessarily intrinsic algebraic types. A Galois group might be cyclic, solvable, or nonabelian simple; a fundamental group might be free or abelian.

## 5. Classification by additional topology or geometry

Here the group carries more than an abstract multiplication table.

| Type | Additional structure |
|---|---|
| Topological group | A topology making multiplication and inversion continuous |
| Discrete group | A group equipped with the discrete topology |
| Locally compact group | A topological group with compact neighborhoods locally |
| Compact group | A topological group whose underlying space is compact |
| Lie group | A smooth manifold with smooth multiplication and inversion |
| Algebraic group | An algebraic-geometric group object with regular group operations |
| Profinite group | An inverse limit of finite groups, carrying its inverse-limit topology |

Topological, Lie, and algebraic groups link group theory to analysis, differential geometry, and algebraic geometry. Profinite groups connect infinite topological groups to their finite quotients. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

Examples clarify the overlap:

- \((\mathbb R,+)\) is abelian and a noncompact Lie group.
- The circle group \(U(1)\) is abelian, compact, and a Lie group.
- \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\) is nonabelian, compact, and a Lie group.
- Any abstract group can be given the discrete topology.

“Finite versus infinite” and “discrete versus nondiscrete” are different distinctions: the former concerns cardinality; the latter concerns topology.

## 6. Other important advanced classification axes

Beyond the preceding map, specialists study many further properties:

| Class | Main idea |
|---|---|
| Residually finite | Every nonidentity element remains nonidentity in some finite quotient |
| Amenable | Admits an invariant mean |
| Hyperbolic | For finitely generated groups, Cayley graphs have uniformly thin geodesic triangles |
| Virtually abelian / nilpotent / free | Has a finite-index subgroup of the indicated type |
| Polycyclic | Has a finite subnormal series with cyclic factors |
| Locally \(P\) | Every finitely generated subgroup has property \(P\) |

These illustrate why no exhaustive taxonomy can be a short list: classification can involve algebraic, geometric, analytic, and algorithmic properties. Geometric group theory in particular studies finitely generated groups through Cayley graphs and large-scale geometry. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

## 7. What does “classify groups” actually mean?

There are two different tasks.

### A. Classify a particular group by its properties

For example, the square symmetry group is:

- finite, of order \(8\);
- nonabelian;
- dihedral;
- a \(2\)-group;
- nilpotent and therefore solvable;
- realizable as permutations of four vertices;
- realizable by orthogonal \(2\times2\) real matrices.

Its geometric description and multiplication structure make several of these classifications visible at once. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics))

### B. Classify an entire class up to isomorphism

This means finding every possible group in the class, with a criterion telling when two descriptions represent the same group.

An isomorphism is a bijection \(\varphi:G\to H\) satisfying

\[
\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b).
\]

Isomorphic groups have the same abstract structure, even if their elements have different names or appearances. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics))

Important classification results include:

#### Cyclic groups

Every cyclic group is isomorphic to either

\[
\mathbb Z
\quad\text{or}\quad
C_n=\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z.
\]

Thus cyclic groups are classified by their order. [math.uci](https://www.math.uci.edu/~ndonalds/math120a/notes.pdf)

#### Finitely generated abelian groups

Every finitely generated abelian group has the form

\[
G\cong
\mathbb Z^r\times C_{n_1}\times\cdots\times C_{n_k},
\]

where \(n_i>1\) and

\[
n_1\mid n_2\mid\cdots\mid n_k.
\]

The rank \(r\) and these invariant factors determine the group up to isomorphism. For finite abelian groups, \(r=0\). This is a complete classification within a restricted class, unlike the general classification problem for abstract groups. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics))

#### Finite simple groups

Every finite simple group belongs to one of four categories:

1. Cyclic groups of prime order.
2. Alternating groups \(A_n\), for \(n\ge5\).
3. Finite simple groups of Lie type.
4. The 26 sporadic simple groups. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

Finite groups of Lie type are finite algebraic families; they should not be confused with real or complex Lie groups.

### Why this does not classify all finite groups

Every finite group has a composition series with simple factors, but the factors alone do not determine the group: one must also understand how they are assembled through group extensions. Subgroups and quotients require additional data to reconstruct the original group. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

For example, both \(C_4\) and \(C_2\times C_2\) have two composition factors isomorphic to \(C_2\), yet they are not isomorphic: \(C_4\) has an element of order \(4\), whereas \(C_2\times C_2\) does not.

For arbitrary finitely presented groups, the limitations are deeper: there is no general algorithm deciding whether two such presentations define isomorphic groups. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)

## 8. A compact mental map

Rather than placing a group into one box, ask:

1. How many elements does it have?
2. How many generators and relations does it need?
3. Do its elements commute?
4. What orders do its elements have?
5. What normal subgroups and quotients does it have?
6. Is it nilpotent, solvable, simple, or perfect?
7. How does it act—as permutations, matrices, or geometric transformations?
8. Does it carry a compatible topology, smooth structure, or algebraic structure?

These questions separate intrinsic structure, concrete realization, and additional structure—the three main perspectives behind group classification. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)
